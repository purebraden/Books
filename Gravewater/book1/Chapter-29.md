# CHAPTER 29

Kael slept for twelve hours and woke up angry at himself for it.

The room was still dim, the curtains drawn tight enough to keep morning
out. The brothel had its own sense of time: nights that ran long,
mornings that arrived late, days that blurred because work here wasn't
measured by bells or sun.

Kael lay still for a moment, listening.

Footsteps in the hall. A laugh downstairs. A door closing. The soft
creak of the building settling into its bones like an old ship.

No shouts. No whistles. No pounding at the door.

No city.

His ankle throbbed as soon as he moved it. The pain was duller now, less
sharp, more like a heavy hand pressing than a blade stabbing. The cut at
his side had scabbed. His shoulder ached where a club had kissed him
days ago.

He sat up slowly and stared at his hands.

They were clean.

That made him uneasy.

Kael swung his legs over the edge of the bed and forced himself to
stand. The ankle protested, but it held. He took two careful steps to
the basin and splashed cold water on his face.

The boy staring back at him in the cracked mirror looked younger than
Kael felt.

Too young for the things he'd done.

Too young for the way the city now remembered him.

He dried his face with the cloth and sat at the small table, unwrapped
the ankle bandage, and examined the swelling. Still there. Still angry.
But not as bright. Not as urgent.

He rewrapped it tighter than comfort wanted.

Comfort was dangerous.

The madam's words from the night before lingered: Keep your hands to
yourself. You understand?

Kael had nodded too quickly.

He hadn't needed telling. He'd needed reminding.

Fifteen.

It was a number that meant nothing on the street and everything in a
room like this.

Downstairs, the house woke slowly. Voices grew. A clink of cups. A low
murmur that sounded like a dozen small lives brushing up against each
other, making warmth out of friction.

Kael stayed in his room until hunger became louder than caution.

When he finally stepped into the hall, he kept his hood down. The
brothel didn't run on anonymity the way the docks did. It ran on
familiarity and boundaries. If you made yourself a ghost, people got
curious. If you acted like a client, no one cared.

He walked down the stairs slowly, one hand on the railing.

The common room smelled like soap and fried dough and perfume layered
over old wood. Women sat at tables drinking tea and laughing. Men
lingered near walls, trying to look like they weren't staring. A guard
stood by the door with arms folded, watching everything and nothing.

The madam---older woman with tired eyes and a spine made of iron---stood
behind a counter and looked up as Kael approached.

She didn't smile.

"You're awake," she said.

Kael nodded. "Food."

She slid a plate across the counter without asking what he

wanted. Bread, eggs, something salty. Real food, not scrap.

Kael hesitated.

She tapped the counter. "Eat. Then go back upstairs if you're going to
sulk."

Kael's face warmed. He took the plate and moved to a corner table with
his back to the wall.

He ate quickly, then forced himself to slow. Forced himself to chew.
Forced himself to taste.

The first bite felt like guilt.

The second felt like relief.

By the third, he was hungry enough to stop pretending he wasn't.

A girl passed his table carrying a tray of cups. She glanced at him,
then smiled---light, playful, the way someone smiles when they've
decided you're interesting but harmless.

"Well," she said, setting cups down at a nearby table, "you're still
here."

Kael swallowed. "I paid."

The girl's smile widened. "That's not what I meant."

Kael stared at his plate like it had offended him. "I'm not going
anywhere."

"Mm," she hummed, and leaned on the back of a chair two tables away,
eyes on him. "You don't look like someone who stays put."

Kael shrugged, too stiff. "I can."

The girl's gaze slid over him---his limp, his tired eyes, the way his
hands hovered close to his body like he expected to need them. Her
expression softened, just slightly, like she'd noticed the part of him
that wasn't pretending.

"You want anything else?" she asked.

Kael's brain produced a dozen wrong answers and jammed them all in his
throat.

He cleared it with a cough. "No."

The girl laughed softly. Not cruel. Just amused.

"Sure," she said, and walked away.

Kael let out a breath he hadn't realized he was holding.

Shy.

That was humiliating.

He could cut a man down in a blink. He could vanish out of a net. He
could walk into a trap and turn it into profit.

But a smile from a girl and his face burned like he was a child caught
stealing sweets.

He hated that the truth of it felt like being exposed.

He finished eating and went back upstairs.

He lasted ten minutes in his room before the stillness started crawling
under his skin. He paced the small space twice, then stopped and sat on
the edge of the bed, fists clenched.

The knowing hovered at the edges of his mind like a restless animal.

Out there, the city was moving.

He could feel it, even through walls.

Not in footsteps---those were constant---but in pattern. In the way the
brothel guards were a touch more alert. In the way the madam's eyes
flicked to the door every time it opened, then relaxed only when she
recognized the face.

The city wasn't hunting.

It was tracking.

And tracking didn't require running.

It required patience.

Kael pressed his palms to his eyes.

He forced himself to rest anyway.

Over the next three days, the brothel became a strange kind of world.

Kael learned its rhythms the way he'd learned patrol schedules.

Morning came late, but it came. Laundry moved in waves. Girls drifted
from room to room like they owned the hallways because they did. Clients
arrived, left, returned. Names weren't asked. Coin was counted.

The madam enforced rules like laws.

No violence inside.

No screaming unless it was paid for and contained.

No underage girls.

No underage boys either---except Kael, who had arrived bleeding and had
paid enough up front to make the problem hers.

She treated him like a nuisance and protected him like an asset.

Kael didn't know how to feel about that.

He stayed mostly upstairs. When he came down, it was for food or water
or to listen.

Listening had always kept him alive.

Here, listening taught him things he didn't want to know.

The first rumor came the second night, drifting up through the common
room like smoke.

A sailor with a face like cracked leather was drinking hard and talking
louder than he meant to.

"---not a storm," he insisted, slamming his cup down. "No wind, no
lightning, nothing. Fog rolled in like it had hands. Then something hit
the hull---once---like a fist the size of a cart. We didn't see it. We
felt it."

A man at the table laughed nervously. "You were drunk."

"I am drunk," the sailor snapped. "I wasn't drunk then. We watched the
water glow under the boat. Like lanterns

beneath the waves. Then the ropes---"

He held up his hands, fingers spread, as if he could show the shape in
air.

"Ropes," he repeated. "Not rope. Not seaweed. Ropes. Thick. Wet. Moving
like they knew where to grab."

Someone cursed.

Someone else crossed themselves.

A girl in a red dress rolled her eyes like she'd heard this story too
many times. "Sailors always see monsters."

The sailor leaned forward, eyes wild. "Then why aren't the ships coming
back?"

Silence hit the table.

Kael, upstairs, froze mid-step.

That line mattered.

He stepped closer to the stairwell and listened harder, heart thudding.

Another voice, lower, more controlled. "It's not just storms. The routes
are wrong. Captains are taking longer paths. Cutting corners. Trying to
save time."

"And dying for it," someone muttered.

The sailor laughed bitterly. "More leave than return. That's the truth.
You can count the masts."

Kael's throat went dry.

He'd always imagined the sea as escape---dangerous, yes, but open. A
place where the city's memory would thin. A place where the rules
changed.

Now the sea sounded like another kind of cage.

He retreated to his room and sat on the bed, staring at the wall.

The knowing hovered.

Kael let it settle into something quieter and sharper---not the flare of
combat, not the rush of vanishing, but the cold

alignment of information.

Ships. Routes. Delays. The boat name in the ledger. The audit window.
The way the city was absorbing him into paper.

Everything was tightening.

Not snapping.

Tightening.

The next day, the girls began teasing him more openly.

Not in a mean way. In the casual way people tease something small and
cute that doesn't bite back.

Kael hated that he didn't bite back.

One of them---dark hair, quick smile---caught him on the stairs.

"You always run upstairs like we're going to eat you," she said, hands
on her hips.

Kael tried to step around her. "I'm busy."

"With what?" she asked, eyebrows raised. "Brooding?"

Kael's face heated. "Yes."

She laughed, delighted. "Honest. That's rare."

Kael muttered, "Move."

She moved---slowly, just enough to make him wait an extra second.

"You're pretty," she said, like it was an observation about weather.

Kael stopped dead.

His brain short-circuited.

He looked away, hard, like he could hide inside the side of his face.

"Don't," he said.

The girl blinked, surprise softening her. "Don't what?"

Kael swallowed. "Don't say things like that."

Her expression changed then---less teasing, more careful.

"How old are you," she asked quietly.

Kael's throat tightened again. The number was a weight.

"Fifteen," he said.

The girl's eyes widened. Her mouth opened, then closed.

She stepped back immediately, like she'd bumped into a boundary she
hadn't seen.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh---sorry. I didn't---"

Kael waved a hand too quickly. "It's fine."

It wasn't fine.

He brushed past her and limped up the stairs, heart racing as if he'd
just escaped a blade.

In his room, he sat down hard on the bed and stared at his hands again.

Fifteen.

The city had turned him into a weapon, but he was still a boy who
couldn't handle a compliment without panic.

That truth felt like a crack in armor he hadn't realized he wore.

That night, the rumors got worse.

A different sailor---this one quieter, voice low and rough---spoke near
the hearth.

"Ship came back," he said. "Not ours. Drifted in without sails. Hull
crushed in from the side like something squeezed it. No bodies. Just
blood on the deck and rope marks on the rail."

"Rope marks?" someone asked.

"Like something hauled men overboard," the sailor said. "And the
water... the water was wrong. Too still. Like it was waiting."

Kael listened from the stairs, breath shallow.

A girl laughed uneasily. "You're all trying to scare each other."

The sailor looked at her with tired eyes. "No, love. We're trying to
figure out if it's worth going out again."

A man at the table muttered, "Worth it for the pay."

The sailor shook his head. "Pay's going up because captains are
desperate. Crew's harder to hire. People don't want to die in fog they
can't fight."

Kael's fingers tightened on the railing.

He knew desperation.

He'd lived in it.

Now he heard it in the voices of men who had always thought the sea was
theirs.

And he couldn't stop thinking about the ledger under his bed. The boat
name written twice. The way it threaded through delays like a stitched
wound.

The brothel was safe.

For now.

But even here, the city seeped in through gossip and rumor, through
sailors' mouths and the madam's tightening stare.

Kael started to notice other things too.

A guard outside the brothel changed. Not the same man every night
anymore. Two guards instead of one. A new face at the door with eyes
that scanned the street longer than necessary.

The madam didn't mention it.

But her jaw set more often.

Kael's rest became uneasy.

He slept, but he woke at sounds that didn't matter. He ate, but food sat
heavy. His ankle improved, but his mind didn't.

By the end of the week, he understood something he hadn't wanted to
accept:

Hiding bought time.

It didn't erase the problem.

The city was still processing him. The auditors were still logging. The
men in gray were still running scripts.

And out beyond the docks, the sea was growing hungry.

Kael stood at his small window one night, curtain pulled back just
enough to see the black water beyond the buildings.

Lanterns bobbed on boats like stars drowning.

Somewhere out there, ships were leaving.

Somewhere out there, ships weren't coming back.

Kael pressed his forehead to the cool glass and closed his eyes.

He wasn't ready to leave yet.

He wasn't safe enough to stay.

And he was fifteen, caught between the boy he'd never been allowed to be
and the thing the city had made him.

Downstairs, laughter rose again---bright, almost normal.

It sounded like a world Kael had never lived in.

He let the sound wash over him anyway, because for a few moments it was
the closest thing to peace he'd had since the crew died.

Then he pulled the curtain shut and went back to bed, knowing the
countdown hadn't stopped---only quieted.

Somewhere beyond the walls, Gravewater kept writing.

And the sea kept eating.
