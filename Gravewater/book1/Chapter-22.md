# CHAPTER 22

Gravewater began paying for sight.

Not with coin—with people.

Lanterns multiplied along the quay.

Mirrors appeared in places that didn't need them.

Men with long poles tested shadows the way fishermen tested water, prodding for movement where none should be.

It didn't help.

A sensation of change as friction.

Movement that once flowed now dragged.

Corners that used to forgive now resisted.

The city had committed resources to looking.

That was expensive.

It also made them predictable.

Kael stopped crossing open ground entirely.

He moved through overlap—awnings, scaffolds, hanging nets, the places where lines of sight fought each other and nobody won.

When he had to cross light, he crossed early, before watchers settled into certainty.

They missed him anyway.

The first confrontation came midafternoon, sharp and professional.

A trio waited at the mouth of a service alley Kael used to avoid crowds.

Two watchers high.

One closer low.

Clean geometry.

They'd studied him. "Kael," the closer said. "We need to talk." Kael didn't stop. "Now," the man added.

Kael turned sideways instead, stepping into the alley's mouth and then not being there anymore—not invisibly, not magically.

Just misaligned.

The watchers above leaned, eyes tracking the wrong angle.

The closer hesitated.

That was enough.

Kael crossed ten feet in a blink, brushed past the man's shoulder, and left a cut behind him that took strength but not life.

The man dropped to a knee, gasping.

Kael didn't finish it.

He was already gone.

The city's answer was to escalate procedure.

By dusk, Watch captains were carrying written orders instead of relying on memory.

Patrols overlapped.

Runners doubled back to confirm messages they'd just delivered.

Everything slowed.

Kael sped up.

He cut channels again—cleaner this time.

Not sabotage.

Redirection.

A warning whispered to the right person at the wrong moment.

A ledger page returned late enough to expose a lie.

A shipment arriving exactly when it shouldn't.

By nightfall, two minor factions were openly accusing each other of interference.

Kael watched from above, chewing dried fruit and counting breaths.

This was the cost of trying to see everything.

The trap came after dark, elegant in its brutality.

A plaza cleared under pretense of maintenance.

Lanterns arranged to flatten shadows.

Watchers posted on every approach.

No obvious exit.

Kael sensed it the instant he stepped onto the stone.

They wanted him here.

He didn't run.

He walked to the center of the plaza and stopped.

A captain stepped forward, flanked by men who had learned not to rush. "You're done," the captain said. "You don't move until we finish talking." Kael looked around—five feet to the nearest lantern, ten to the column, fifteen to the street mouth.

All watched.

He nodded once.

Then he moved.

slow.

Sideways.

He crossed into the lantern's spill and then out of it in the same step, timing his movement to the captain's blink.

The watchers swore they saw him go left.

He went right.

A shout went up.

Boots scraped stone.

Orders collided.

Kael slipped through the blind angle the lanterns couldn't cover—the space between intention and execution.

He was fifteen feet away before anyone realized the plaza was empty.

The captain cursed.

Kael didn't stop until the harbor wind hit his face.

He climbed, crossed, vanished, reappeared—movement becoming a language the city struggled to parse.

By the time the Watch sealed the plaza, Kael was already watching from a roof two streets away.

They'd spent a fortune on sight.

It had bought them nothing.

Kael sat with his knees drawn up, eyes on the black water beyond the docks.

Somewhere, someone important would be tallying costs and deciding that procedure had failed.

That decision wouldn't be quiet.

Not anymore.

He exhaled slowly and felt the pressure gather—not inside him, but around him.

The city's gaze was sharpening into intent.

One more step.

One more mistake.

And the way Gravewater looked at him would change forever.
