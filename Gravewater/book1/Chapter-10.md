# CHAPTER 10

The stairs ended at a door that had never been meant for feet.

It was wide, reinforced, and scarred with old impact marks that had been
patched rather than repaired. Kael paused at the threshold, listening.
Beyond it, the air carried the smell of dry wood and old ink---warehouse
space, not dockside. The pressure behind his eyes tightened, not in
warning but in recognition.

This place mattered.

Kael pushed the door open.

The warehouse beyond was lit evenly, deliberately. No shadows to hide
in. No bright glare either. Just enough light to see everything clearly,
including how little room there was to maneuver. Crates were stacked in
neat, labeled rows. Ledgers sat on tables, open and waiting. Two men
stood near the far wall, hands folded, faces empty of expression.

And one woman waited at the center table.

She looked up as Kael entered, expression calm, almost welcoming. She
was dressed plainly---dark coat, no visible insignia---but everything
about her posture suggested ownership of the space. Not authority taken
by force. Authority granted by function.

"Kael," she said. "Thank you for coming."

Kael didn't answer. He stepped fully inside, letting the door close
behind him with a soft, final sound.

"You weren't invited," he said.

The woman smiled faintly. "You were guided."

Kael's jaw tightened. He felt the knowing stir, eager, insistent. He
held it back with effort.

"Sit," she said, gesturing to the chair opposite her.

Kael didn't.

"That's all right," she continued smoothly. "We can talk like this."

Kael glanced at the men by the wall. They didn't move. Didn't need to.

"What do you want?" Kael asked.

The woman tilted her head, considering. "Efficiency."

Kael felt a faint chill. "I don't have any to spare."

She smiled again, as if amused by something only she could see. "That's
not true. You've been remarkably efficient these last two days. Avoiding
patrols. Redirecting attention. Making choices that minimize immediate
damage."

Kael's stomach tightened. "You've been watching."

"We all have," she replied. "Just not all at once."

The knowing surged despite his resistance, drawn to the certainty in her
voice. Kael let it open just enough to read the edges of her intent.

Pain lanced behind his eyes.

Truth unfolded brutally. The woman was telling the truth---but not all
of it. She wasn't the source of the attention. She was a conduit. A
functionary. Replaceable.

Behind her, Kael sensed layers upon layers of obligation and authority
rising upward through the city's invisible structure, like scaffolding
disappearing into fog.

He staggered slightly and caught himself on the edge of the table.

"Careful," the woman said mildly. "You don't want to overexert
yourself."

Kael glared at her. "You know what I can do."

She nodded. "We know what you can't do, too."

That landed harder.

Kael forced his breathing steady. "You brought me here to

threaten me."

"No," she said. "We brought you here to clarify."

She reached into a drawer and placed an object on the table between
them.

The ledger.

Wrapped exactly as Kael had left it. No damage. No sign it had been
opened.

Kael's pulse spiked. "How did you---"

"We know where things belong," the woman said. "And where they don't."

The knowing screamed, recognizing the ledger's weight not just as paper
and ink but as a node in a vast accounting system. Kael felt its
connections light up painfully---threads running from it into the city's
bones, down into the water below, upward into hands that never touched
it directly.

"This isn't theft," the woman continued. "It's misplacement."

Kael laughed, harsh and brittle. "You killed people for misplacement."

The woman's expression didn't change. "People die when balances shift
too quickly."

Kael felt something cold settle in his gut. "You're feeding it."

The woman didn't deny it.

"The city produces excess," she said calmly. "Fear. Debt. Violence.
Neglect. It has to go somewhere."

Kael's head throbbed violently as the knowing connected the pieces he'd
already sensed. "So you dump it."

"We redistribute," she corrected. "We manage."

Kael slammed his hand on the table, pain flaring through his skull as
the knowing reacted to his anger. "You're sacrificing people."

The woman met his gaze evenly. "We're preventing collapse."

Silence stretched between them, heavy with implication.

"What happens if you stop?" Kael demanded.

The woman considered him carefully. "You saw what's below," she said.
"You understand what it absorbs."

Kael swallowed. He thought of the water pulling inward. Of iron bands
humming under strain. Of weight being borne so something worse didn't
rise.

"If we stop," she continued, "Gravewater doesn't sink slowly anymore. It
fails all at once."

Kael felt sick.

"So what?" he said. "You want me to help you?"

The woman shook her head. "No. We want you to stop interfering."

She slid the ledger an inch closer to him.

"You return this to us," she said. "You walk away. We give you time.
Space. Protection, if necessary."

Kael stared at the ledger. His hands trembled.

"And if I don't?"

The woman's smile faded. "Then you remain an inefficiency."

Kael laughed again, quieter this time. "You already took everything I
had."

The woman's gaze softened---just a fraction. "No," she said. "You still
have choice. That's rare."

The knowing pulsed hard, showing Kael the truth beneath her words: the
offer was real, but poisoned. Acceptance would bind him into the system
permanently. Refusal would mark him for removal.

There was no safe answer.

Kael stepped back from the table. "I won't be part of this."

The woman nodded, unsurprised. "I didn't think you would."

One of the men by the wall shifted slightly. Not threatening. Just
present.

"We'll give you time," the woman said. "Not much."

She gestured toward the door. "Leave."

Kael hesitated only a moment before turning away. His head pounded,
vision blurring at the edges as the knowing fought him, desperate to
keep mapping, keep understanding.

He forced it down and walked.

The door closed behind him.

Kael leaned against the wall in the corridor outside, breathing hard,
heart hammering. The warehouse behind him felt less like a place and
more like a mouth that had chosen not to close.

He understood now, with sickening clarity:

Gravewater was not broken.

It was maintained.

And by seeing the truth, Kael had made himself incompatible with it.

He pushed away from the wall and started moving again, deeper into the
city's understructure, knowing that whatever came next would not be
accidental.
