# CHAPTER 16

Kael did nothing at first.

That was the hardest part.

He held still in the harness, muscles shaking, breath shallow, letting
the room continue as it had been trained to continue. The workers moved
in their familiar loops---check, record, adjust, repeat. No one looked
at him directly anymore. He was present in the way a column was present.
Necessary. Unremarkable.

That was the weakness.

Procedure depended on expectation.

Kael waited until the gray-haired woman stepped away from the gauges to
confer with another worker. He waited until the younger man took her
place, eyes flicking between dials with practiced anxiety. He waited
until the hum of the chamber

settled into its most stable register---the sound it made when no one
expected anything to change.

Only then did Kael move.

Not much. Just enough.

He shifted his weight by a fraction, letting the harness take it, then
eased it back. The change was subtle enough that the gauges didn't
spike---just trembled. The younger worker frowned, tapped the glass of
one dial, and made a small adjustment.

Exactly as Kael needed.

The knowing pressed in, heavy but controlled, not showing him truths so
much as tolerances. He could feel the margins---how much variance the
procedure allowed before escalation. How long an anomaly could persist
before it became someone's responsibility.

Responsibility was the bottleneck.

Kael repeated the movement minutes later. A little more this time.
Enough to require a note.

The worker scribbled something on a slate and glanced toward the
corridor that led upward, as if expecting someone to appear and tell him
what to do.

No one came.

Kael felt sweat run down his spine. His arms trembled harder now, pain
flaring where the harness bit into him. The knowing throbbed dully, like
a bruise pressed too often.

He shifted again.

This time, the gray-haired woman noticed.

"Hold," she snapped.

Kael froze.

The gauges steadied. The hum settled.

She stared at the readouts, lips pressed thin. "That shouldn't have
fluctuated."

Kael said nothing. He kept his breathing shallow, controlled.

The woman looked at him---not accusing, not suspicious. Assessing.

"Are you slipping?" she asked.

Kael swallowed. "I'm tired."

That, at least, was allowed.

She nodded once. "We'll need to log it."

Log it.

Not stop it. Not change course. Record deviation and move on.

The knowing pulsed---this was the opening.

Kael waited until she turned away again, attention pulled by another
worker calling out a minor discrepancy on the far side of the chamber.

Then he acted.

He didn't touch the levers. He didn't interfere with the chains. He did
the only thing the procedure hadn't accounted for.

He spoke.

"If I go down," Kael said hoarsely, loud enough to carry, "you won't
have time to compensate."

The room stilled.

Every worker turned toward him.

Kael felt his heart hammering against his ribs. The knowing surged
painfully, mapping reactions, predicting responses, showing him exactly
how fragile this moment was.

The gray-haired woman stepped closer. "What are you doing?"

"Explaining," Kael said. "What happens if I lose consciousness. Or
muscle control. Or balance."

She stared at him. "That won't happen."

Kael laughed, sharp and humorless. "It already is."

As if on cue, his arms shook violently. Pain lanced through his
shoulders. He let a small sound escape---real, uncontrolled.

The younger worker swore under his breath.

Kael pressed on. "Your procedure assumes a static load. I'm not static.
I'm exhausted. And if I fail, you don't get a warning spike. You get a
cascade."

The gray-haired woman hesitated.

That hesitation was everything.

She looked at the gauges again, then at Kael, then at the corridor
leading upward.

"Who authorized this duration?" she demanded.

No one answered.

Because no one had.

It was the gap Kael needed.

"You don't have a replacement," Kael said quietly. "You're hoping delay
solves it. It won't."

Silence stretched.

The knowing burned behind his eyes, screaming at him to hold, to
maintain alignment, to not let the load slip while he talked.

Kael felt something tear in his shoulder. He bit back a cry.

The gray-haired woman cursed softly.

"Get the slate," she snapped to the younger worker. "Now."

The worker ran.

This was it.

Not escape. Escalation.

The woman looked back at Kael. "If I disengage you without clearance---"

"You'll write a report," Kael said. "And someone above you will have to
acknowledge it."

She stared at him, eyes sharp now. "You planned this."

Kael swallowed. "I planned for you to notice."

The slate came back. The woman skimmed it quickly, jaw tight.

She made a decision---not a moral one. A procedural one.

"All right," she said. "Temporary redistribution. Five minutes."

Five minutes was an eternity.

She barked orders. Workers moved fast now, urgency snapping into place
where habit had been. Chains adjusted. Counterweights shifted---not
smoothly, but adequately. The chamber groaned in protest.

Kael felt the load ease slightly.

"Unhook him," the woman said.

Hands grabbed the harness. The release mechanism stuck for a terrifying
second, then gave way with a harsh metallic snap.

Kael collapsed forward, barely caught by two workers who weren't
expecting his full weight.

The hum spiked, then steadied again---louder, rougher, but holding.

Kael's vision swam. He gasped, lungs burning, every muscle screaming as
blood rushed back into places that had been locked rigid for too long.

"Move him," the woman said. "Now."

They half-carried, half-dragged him toward the side corridor. Kael's
boots scraped uselessly. His head pounded so hard he thought he might
black out.

Behind him, someone shouted as another gauge wavered.

"Go!" the woman snapped. "Before this becomes another report no one
reads!"

They shoved Kael through the corridor and dumped him against the wall
just outside the chamber. The door slammed

shut behind them, sealing with a heavy clang.

Kael slid to the floor, shaking uncontrollably.

He was free.

Not safe. Not hidden.

But no longer load-bearing.

The knowing pulsed weakly, exhausted, showing him what he already
understood:

Reports would be written. Names would be attached. Questions would move
upward.

Delay had bought him minutes---maybe hours.

No more.

Kael dragged himself to his feet, leaning heavily against the wall. His
body felt broken. His head throbbed. His hands wouldn't stop shaking.

He had forced the system to acknowledge him.

Now it would respond the only way human systems ever did.

By assigning blame.

And finding him.

He pushed off the wall and staggered down the corridor, deeper into the
levels that were never meant for escape, only access.

The story was moving again.

And it was no longer pretending he was invisible.
