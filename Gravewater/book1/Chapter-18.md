# CHAPTER 18

By the time Kael reached the mid-tier markets again, Gravewater was
already telling stories about him.

He heard them in fragments as he moved---half-sentences tossed between
vendors, whispers sliding under the clang of crate hooks and the slap of
wet boards. A boy had slipped the Watch. A thief had come up from the
lower levels with blood on his sleeve. A demon-eyed runner had made men
choke on their own breath. Someone said he'd killed two Watchmen with a
look. Someone else said he'd stolen a ledger that didn't belong to
anyone human.

Most of it was wrong.

None of it helped.

Kael kept his hood low and his head down, moving with the current of
bodies like driftwood pretending it chose its path. His ankle was
swollen now; every step landed like a hammer. He had wrapped it with a
strip torn from his shirt, tight enough to keep it from folding but not
tight enough to stop the pain.

Pain was fine.

Attention wasn't.

A Watch patrol passed two walkways over, lanterns up even in daylight.
Kael watched them from the corner of his eye and forced himself to keep
walking. Running would draw eyes. Walking hurt less than being seen.

A vendor shouted the price of shellfish. A woman argued over a measure
of salt. A child darted between knees, laughing. Normal life moved
around Kael like he was invisible.

He needed that invisibility.

Then he heard his own name.

Not shouted.

Spoken, casually, as if it belonged in ordinary conversation.

"...Kael. That's what they're calling him."

Kael's stomach tightened.

He turned down a narrow side lane without thinking, slipping past
stacked barrels and hanging nets. The sounds of the market dulled behind
him. The lane smelled of damp rope and old vinegar.

He stopped under an awning and listened.

Footsteps approached from the far end---unhurried, not Watch boots.
Multiple sets. Controlled spacing.

Kael's body tensed. The knowing stirred faintly, pressing at the edges
of his perception, offering clarity.

He forced it down.

He didn't want clarity.

He wanted out.

Kael moved again, limping faster than his ankle wanted, and turned into
a passage that cut behind a row of storage sheds. He'd used it years ago
to avoid a creditor. It was narrow enough that two people couldn't pass
without brushing.

Halfway through, a figure stepped into the far end and filled it.

Kael stopped.

The man was broad-shouldered, coat open, hands visible at his sides. Not
Watch. No lantern. No uniform. Just a face that belonged to the
docks---hard lines, calm eyes, the kind of posture that said I don't
need to rush.

Behind Kael, someone stepped into the other end of the passage.

Kael didn't turn. He didn't have to. He could feel the presence.

Trapped.

The man ahead smiled slightly. "Kael."

Kael's mouth went dry. "I don't know you."

"You don't have to," the man said. "I know enough."

Kael kept his hands near his sides, fingers flexing. His body wanted to
run. There was nowhere to run.

"What do you want?" Kael asked.

"Conversation," the man replied. "And maybe a favor. Depends how polite
you are."

Kael's jaw tightened. "I don't have favors."

The man chuckled, not amused---testing. "Everyone has favors. Even the
ones who think they don't."

Kael glanced past the man's shoulder. The lane beyond was open,
brighter. Freedom twenty feet away, sealed by one person and a calm
voice.

The knowing pressed harder, urging Kael to look and know the man's
intent.

Kael swallowed and refused.

That refusal cost him immediately.

Because he missed the tiny shift in the man's stance---the way his
weight settled, ready to move if Kael tried to bolt.

Kael took a half-step forward anyway, testing.

The man's smile didn't change, but his eyes sharpened. "Don't."

Kael stopped.

A voice behind Kael spoke, close and soft. "He's quick, they said."

Kael turned his head just enough to see the second figure: a woman,
lean, hair tied back, eyes bright with interest. Not fear. Not malice.
Curiosity.

That was worse.

"You're not Watch," Kael said again, more firmly.

"No," the man in front agreed. "We're sensible."

Kael's throat tightened. "Sensible people don't corner a starving kid."

The woman behind him laughed quietly. "Starving? He looks like he
crawled out of the drains."

Kael felt heat rise in his chest---anger, humiliation, exhaustion. He
steadied his breath. "Who sent you?"

"No one sent us," the man said. "We heard."

Kael's mouth went cold. "Heard what?"

"That the Watch is clumsy today," the man said. "That a boy slipped
them. That something happened down below. That people died. That there's
paper involved."

Kael didn't react. He couldn't afford to.

The man continued smoothly, as if listing weather. "And we heard a name.
So we came to see if the name was attached to anything worth buying."

Kael's stomach churned. "I'm not for sale."

The woman behind him leaned in slightly. "Everything is," she murmured.
"It's just a question of price."

Kael forced his voice steady. "I don't have anything."

The man's eyes stayed on Kael's face. "That's the lie, isn't it?"

Kael felt the knowing surge---look, it urged, see what he knows, see
what he wants.

Kael clenched his fists. He did not open himself to it.

He made a different choice.

He bluffed.

"You're late," Kael said flatly. "Whatever you think you heard? It's
already old."

The man's eyebrows lifted slightly. "Is it."

Kael nodded toward the open lane behind the man. "If you're smart, you
let me go. The Watch is looking for a scapegoat, and you're standing in
a perfect alley holding one."

The woman behind Kael clicked her tongue. "He's got teeth."

The man smiled again, a fraction wider. "He's got fear, too."

Kael's ankle throbbed violently. He shifted his weight, and

pain flashed bright enough to make his vision blur.

The man noticed.

His gaze dropped to Kael's wrapped ankle. "You're hurt."

Kael didn't answer.

The man's voice stayed calm. "That means you can't outrun what's coming.
So you can either keep bleeding into alleys until the Watch catches
you---"

"Or?" Kael snapped.

"Or you take shelter," the man said. "With people who aren't interested
in your guilt. Just your usefulness."

Kael's stomach tightened. The offer wasn't kindness. It was a hook.

Kael shook his head. "No."

The man's smile faded just slightly. "You should think before you
refuse."

"I did," Kael said. "I'm thinking right now."

The woman behind him sighed. "He doesn't understand leverage."

Kael's skin crawled. He could feel the trap closing tighter---not
physically, but socially. They weren't going to stab him. They were
going to steer him. Corner him with options that weren't options.

Kael's mind raced.

He could scream and draw Watch attention. Bad. He could fight. He was
hurt, outnumbered. Worse. He could submit. That was the worst of all.

The knowing pressed again, harder now, as if sensing his desperation.

Kael made a mistake.

He opened it.

Just a crack.

Truth slammed into him like cold water. The man's intent was simple:
acquire Kael, deliver him to someone higher up the ladder of dock power,
collect payment or favor. The woman behind Kael wasn't backup; she was
witness---someone meant to confirm the transfer, reduce dispute. The
entire encounter was designed to be clean and deniable.

Kael's head exploded with pain. He gasped, vision flashing white. His
stomach lurched violently.

He shut it down too late.

The man noticed immediately---Kael's flinch, the sudden pallor, the
involuntary breath.

The man's eyes narrowed. "There," he murmured. "That. That's what we
heard about."

Kael's knees threatened to buckle. He forced himself upright, swallowing
bile and blood.

The woman behind him spoke softly, almost delighted. "So it's true."

Kael's voice came out ragged. "Move."

Neither of them did.

The man leaned forward a fraction. "Kael. Listen carefully. I'm going to
make this simple. You come with us, and you don't get turned over to the
Watch. You don't come with us---"

A whistle shrieked nearby.

Close.

All three of them froze for a heartbeat.

Then the man smiled again, but now it was sharp. "---and you get caught
anyway. Except this time you won't be caught alone."

Kael's blood ran cold.

He understood: they would step aside. Let the Watch take him. And if
questioned, they'd say they'd tried to stop him. Or say nothing at all.
Either way, Kael became a problem that

removed itself, and they stayed clean.

The whistle sounded again, closer still. Boots thundered on boards.

Kael's panic rose like bile.

He looked past the man toward the open lane---freedom blocked by calm
confidence---and realized the only way through was to break their
certainty.

Kael took one limping step forward.

The man shifted to block him.

Kael didn't fight him.

He shoved past the woman behind him instead---slamming his shoulder into
her, driving her into the wall. She yelped, startled more than hurt. The
man cursed and turned, momentarily wrong-footed by the move.

Kael ran.

Not fast.

Just desperate.

He burst out of the passage into the wider lane---and immediately saw
Watchmen at the far end, lanterns up, faces turning toward him like dogs
catching scent.

"THERE!" someone shouted.

Kael's heart slammed. He limped hard left, cutting between stalls,
knocking over a stack of baskets. People shouted. Someone screamed.
Chaos blossomed.

Behind him, the dock man's voice rose, clear and cutting: "KAEL! HE'S
HERE!"

Kael felt something inside him sink.

They weren't just hunting him now.

They were naming him.

He shoved through the crowd, pain shredding his ankle, breath ragged,
and vanished into a stairwell just as

Watchmen surged onto the lane.

As he climbed, he heard a Watchman barking orders: "Put word out---he's
injured---mid-tiers---name's Kael---find him!"

Kael's throat tightened. He tasted blood.

The city was no longer guessing.

It was organizing.

And somewhere, beyond the Watch and beyond dock opportunists, someone
would hear that name and understand it mattered.

Kael reached the top of the stairwell and stumbled out onto a narrow
bridge, breath tearing.

He didn't slow until he was out of sight.

Only then did he stop, hunched over the railing, shaking, and realize
the worst part:

He had used the knowing.

And they had noticed.
