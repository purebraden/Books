# CHAPTER 4

The first door didn't open.

It should have. Kael had used it a dozen times over the years---a narrow
service entry tucked between a cooper's shop and a shuttered alehouse,
its latch worn smooth by hands that knew where to press. It had never
been locked, not truly. It existed in that category of Gravewater
architecture that survived because everyone agreed not to draw attention
to it.

Kael pressed the latch.

Nothing.

He tried again, slower this time, fingers feeling for the familiar give.
The wood was damp, swollen just enough to change the tolerances. Or it
had been reinforced. Either way, it did not yield.

Behind him, Tessa exhaled sharply. "That's new."

Bram stepped closer, eyes scanning the street. "We're exposed."

Kael nodded and stepped back, letting the door remain closed. Doors that
refused to open usually had reasons, and most of those reasons involved
people watching to see what you'd do next.

They moved on without comment, turning down a side route that ran closer
to the water than Kael preferred. The channel below was higher than it
should have been at this hour, lapping at the pylons with an irregular
rhythm that set his teeth on edge. The city smelled sharper here---salt
and wet iron, like something had been disturbed and not settled back
into place.

Tessa took the lead again, her pace quickening. She didn't like detours.
Detours meant variables, and variables were where people got hurt.

"This is bad timing," she muttered.

"It's intentional timing," Bram replied.

Kael didn't add anything. He was busy noticing how sound carried.
Footsteps echoed farther than they should have, bouncing off stone and
water alike, blurring direction. Voices from two levels up drifted down
with unnatural clarity, words flattening as they traveled.

Gravewater was listening.

They reached the corner where a runner usually waited---an old man with
a permanent limp and a habit of asking questions he already knew the
answers to. He wasn't there.

Kael stopped. So did the others.

Tessa frowned. "He doesn't miss."

"No," Bram agreed. "He leaves."

They stood there for a moment longer than comfort allowed, the absence
stretching thin. Kael felt the pressure behind his eyes begin to gather,
not pain yet, just a sense of wrongness tightening like a drawn cord.

"We don't use him," Kael said.

Tessa shot him a look. "That was our fastest route."

"And now it isn't," Kael replied.

She didn't argue, but her jaw set in a way Kael recognized. Speed was
her answer to most problems. It always had been.

They adjusted course again, heading upward toward a level that smelled
faintly of old soap and hot metal. Kael hated moving up when things went
wrong. The higher levels pretended to be cleaner, safer, more
stable---but they were also watched more closely. People noticed who
didn't belong.

A Watch patrol rounded the corner ahead of them, three men walking too
slowly for their route to be casual. Lanterns hung low, casting light
that seemed to cling to surfaces longer than it should.

Tessa swore under her breath.

"Split," Bram said quietly.

Kael shook his head. "Not yet."

They slowed, becoming just another trio moving with the city's flow. The
patrol passed close enough that Kael could see the scuff marks on one
man's boots---fresh, the wrong kind for this level. The Watchman glanced
at Kael's face, held the look a fraction too long, then moved on.

Tessa didn't breathe until they were clear. "They're interrupting, not
intercepting."

"Testing," Bram said. "Seeing how we react."

Kael felt the faintest surge behind his eyes and pushed it down. He
didn't need to know why yet. He just needed to survive the when.

They ducked into a narrow stairwell that sloped downward and then
leveled out, leading to a small platform overlooking a wider channel.
The water here moved sluggishly, thick with debris, reflecting light in
broken patterns that made Kael's head ache.

"This is where we decide," Tessa said, turning on him. "We can't keep
drifting."

Kael met her gaze. "We don't have enough information."

"We never do," she snapped. "That's not new."

Bram stayed silent, but his eyes moved between them, tracking the shift.

Tessa gestured sharply. "We move the ledger. Now. Somewhere deeper.
Somewhere they won't follow."

Kael felt a chill run through him. "Deeper isn't safer."

"It's quieter," she said.

"Quieter is worse," Kael replied. "It means fewer witnesses."

Tessa's mouth twisted. "You're stalling."

Kael didn't deny it. He looked past her at the water, at the way it
seemed to pull inward near the center of the channel, as if

something below was drawing it down.

"We don't know who's pushing," Kael said. "Moving it blind is how we
lose control entirely."

Tessa laughed, sharp and humorless. "Control's already gone. Mirek's
dead."

The words landed hard.

Bram stepped between them, voice low. "Enough."

Tessa turned away, hands clenched at her sides. Kael felt the familiar
guilt flare---too late, too slow, too careful. He hated that part of
himself even as it kept them alive.

A shout echoed from somewhere nearby. Not alarmed. Directed.

Kael's head snapped up. The pressure behind his eyes spiked briefly,
then settled, leaving him with the unsettling sense of weight---like the
city had shifted and not finished settling.

"Move," Bram said.

They took a narrow bridge that creaked underfoot, the boards flexing
more than Kael remembered. Halfway across, Tessa paused, crouching to
examine something near the railing.

"Waterline's wrong," she said. "Too high."

Kael knelt beside her. She was right. The dark stain along the wood
marked a tide that hadn't been scheduled for hours.

"That doesn't make sense," Bram said.

Kael straightened slowly. "It doesn't have to."

They crossed quickly after that, the sense of being watched sharpening
with every step. Locals avoided their eyes, stepping aside without being
asked. A woman hauling nets took one look at them and changed direction
entirely.

Kael felt the urge to understand rise again, stronger this time. To
reach for whatever part of him had been cataloguing imbalance since
childhood and demand answers.

He refused.

They reached a familiar junction near the old maintenance
platforms---one of the last places that still felt like theirs. Even
here, something was off. The usual noise was muted, as if sound were
being swallowed.

"This place feels wrong," Tessa said quietly.

Kael nodded. "We don't linger."

As they moved, Kael became aware of a sensation he couldn't name at
first---a subtle downward pull, not on his body but on his attention.
His gaze kept drifting toward the gaps between platforms, toward the
dark water below.

He forced himself to look away.

"We're running out of options," Bram said.

Kael didn't argue. He felt it too. Safe ground was shrinking, routes
collapsing not all at once but in increments too small to ignore.

They reached the edge of the maintenance zone and stopped, the city
spreading out below them in a tangle of lights and water.

Tessa spoke without turning. "Next move decides everything."

Kael closed his eyes briefly. The pressure behind them pulsed, heavier
now, as if something deep below Gravewater had shifted its weight.

"We don't go deeper," Kael said. "Not yet."

Tessa looked at him, something like disbelief crossing her face. "Then
what?"

Kael opened his eyes and stared down into the dark. "We find out what's
pressing up."

The words felt dangerous even as he spoke them.

The city creaked in answer, wood and stone adjusting under unseen
strain.

Below them, the water moved---slow, patient, and slightly out of time.
