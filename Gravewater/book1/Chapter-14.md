# CHAPTER 14

The corridor narrowed until Kael had to turn his shoulders sideways to
pass.

The iron bands along the walls were closer together here, bolted so
densely that they resembled scales. They hummed faintly---not
constantly, but in pulses, as if responding to something deeper than
sound. Kael felt the vibration through his boots and into his bones, a
slow rhythm that matched neither his breathing nor his heart.

The knowing pressed down on him like a hand between his shoulder blades.

Not urging him forward.

Measuring him.

He moved carefully, each step deliberate. The pain behind his eyes had
changed quality---not sharper, not more intense, but heavier, as if his
skull were bearing weight rather than heat. When he passed beneath a
thick arch of reinforced beams, the pressure deepened, then eased again
as he cleared it.

The system reacted to his presence.

Kael swallowed and kept going.

Ahead, the corridor opened into another chamber---smaller than the great
maintenance hall above, but more precise. This space had been built by
people who understood exactly what they were asking it to do. Thick
pylons anchored the floor, disappearing through iron grates into the
water below. Between them ran chains, counterbalanced by massive

drums inset into the walls.

And people.

Four workers stood at a control station along one side, hands on levers,
eyes fixed on gauges that twitched in small, constant increments. They
froze as Kael entered.

No one shouted.

No one reached for a weapon.

One of the workers---a woman with hair gone almost entirely
gray---exhaled slowly. "You're not on the slate."

Kael didn't answer. His mouth felt dry.

The knowing stirred, and he let it open just enough to read the room.

The workers' fear was real, but muted---contained by habit. Their
obligations ran upward into the same invisible structure Kael had sensed
before, but here the lines were thicker, heavier. These people didn't
just obey; they maintained.

"We've got a gap," another worker said quietly, eyes flicking to a gauge
that trembled too far toward red. "Shift replacement never arrived."

The gray-haired woman glanced at Kael again, then back to the gauge. "Of
course they didn't."

Kael felt the knowing throb sharply, a warning.

He stepped back instinctively. "I'm not---"

A deep groan rolled through the chamber, vibrating the iron bands. The
floor shuddered. One of the gauges spiked violently, its needle slamming
against the stop with a sharp clack.

The gray-haired woman swore. "We're slipping."

Kael's vision blurred as the knowing flared, flooding him with
structural awareness. He saw pressure building below---water pulling
inward harder than before, iron straining,

counterweights shifting out of balance.

He saw the absence.

A missing load.

The system wasn't failing because it was broken.

It was failing because someone wasn't there.

"Get back," Kael said hoarsely, backing toward the exit.

The woman shook her head. "If we disengage now, the surge will propagate
upward. Mid-tiers won't hold."

Kael stared at her. "Then disengage somewhere else."

She met his gaze evenly. "There is nowhere else."

Another groan rippled through the chamber. The hum deepened, vibrating
Kael's teeth.

The knowing pressed harder, heavier than it ever had before. It wasn't
showing him lies or intentions now. It was showing him load---where
weight should be, where it wasn't, and what would happen if it remained
absent.

A human-shaped absence.

Kael's stomach lurched. "You want me to---"

"We want someone to," the woman said, not unkindly. "You're here."

Kael laughed weakly. "That's not a qualification."

One of the other workers glanced between them, panic creeping into his
voice. "The bracket won't hold much longer."

The gray-haired woman looked at Kael again. Her eyes were tired, not
cruel. "You can leave," she said. "No one will stop you."

The knowing flared brutally at her words, showing Kael the truth beneath
them.

If he left, the system would compensate---violently. The surge would
ripple upward, snapping weaker supports, drowning workers who never even
saw the water move wrong. The city would call it an accident.

Kael staggered, gripping a pylon to stay upright. His head felt like it
was splitting open from the inside, pressure and pain grinding together.

He didn't want this.

He didn't want to be part of it.

But the city had already decided.

"Where?" Kael asked quietly.

The woman gestured toward a narrow platform extending over the grating.
A harness hung there, heavy and old, leather darkened by sweat and
water. Chains ran from it into the wall, disappearing into the machinery
beyond.

"Just long enough to stabilize," she said. "Until the next shift
arrives."

Kael stared at the harness.

The knowing surged again, heavier than before, showing him the truth
without mercy: long enough was a variable the system adjusted when
convenient.

Kael's hands trembled as he stepped onto the platform. The iron grating
beneath his boots vibrated, responding to his weight.

The hum shifted.

The gauges steadied slightly.

Everyone in the room felt it.

Kael swallowed hard. "I don't know what I'm doing."

The woman shook her head. "Neither did the last one."

Kael flinched. "What happened to them?"

She didn't answer.

That was answer enough.

Kael slipped the harness over his shoulders, the leather stiff and cold
against his skin. The chains clinked softly as he adjusted it, sound
swallowed quickly by the chamber's

steady hum.

The knowing pressed down on him like a physical thing now, aligning with
the machinery, sinking into the same rhythm. Kael felt himself
register---not as a person, but as mass. Load. Resistance.

The platform shuddered, then steadied.

The gauges eased further toward green.

A collective breath went through the room.

Kael closed his eyes, nausea rolling through him. His head pounded
mercilessly, pain blooming and receding in time with the hum. He felt
the water below pulling, testing, as if curious about this new weight it
could feel above.

"Stay still," the woman said gently. "Let the system adjust."

Kael laughed once, hollow. "That's been the story all along, hasn't it?"

She didn't deny it.

Minutes passed. Or seconds. Kael couldn't tell. Time felt distorted
here, stretched thin by pressure and pain.

Slowly, the machinery settled into a new equilibrium. The hum softened.
The gauges stabilized.

Kael opened his eyes and saw the workers watching him---not with
hostility, not even pity, but with a weary gratitude that made his
stomach twist.

The city had accepted him.

He felt it then, unmistakably: his presence reduced strain elsewhere.
Somewhere above, a brace stopped groaning. Somewhere below, the water's
pull eased just a fraction.

Leaving now would undo that.

Leaving would cost lives.

Kael sagged against the harness, exhaustion crashing over him like a
wave. He was shaking uncontrollably now,

muscles screaming from tension he hadn't realized he was holding.

"I didn't agree to this," he whispered.

The knowing pulsed, heavy and undeniable.

Consent had never been part of the equation.

The gray-haired woman met his gaze. "We'll call for replacement," she
said. "As soon as we can."

Kael believed her.

He also knew the system might decide he was efficient.

He stared out over the grating, down into the darkness where the water
moved wrong, and felt the terrible truth settle in his bones:

The city didn't just want him contained.

It wanted him useful.

And for the first time since Tessa died, Kael didn't know how to leave
without killing someone else in his place.
