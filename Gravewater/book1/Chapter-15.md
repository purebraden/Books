# CHAPTER 15

At first, Kael told himself it would be minutes.

He watched the gauges. He listened to the hum. He counted breaths to
keep the knowing from opening wider than he could survive. The workers
moved around him with efficient care, checking chains, adjusting levers,
making small corrections that kept the entire chamber from slipping into
catastrophe.

Minutes, he insisted. Until the next shift.

Then the gray-haired woman came back with a cup of water.

Not offered like charity. Not presented like comfort. Placed on a crate
within reach of his bound hands, as if fueling a machine that had begun
to run hot.

"Drink," she said.

Kael stared at the cup.

His throat burned with thirst. His mouth tasted like blood and iron. He
wanted to refuse out of spite.

He drank anyway.

The water was stale and faintly metallic, but it soothed the rawness
enough that he could breathe without tasting copper.

"How long?" Kael asked, voice hoarse.

The woman looked at a gauge, then at him. "As long as it takes."

Kael laughed once, quietly. "That's not an answer."

She didn't disagree. She simply moved away, returning to her station
like a person returning to duty.

Time blurred.

It didn't pass in clean segments down here. It accumulated like
damp---slow, clinging, thickening in seams until suddenly everything
felt heavier than before. The chamber's light never changed. The hum
never stopped. The water below never ceased its wrong, patient pull.

Kael's muscles began to cramp within an hour. His shoulders burned where
the harness bit into him. His legs trembled from holding still against
the subtle sway of counterweights overhead. The system demanded
steadiness, and his body was not designed to be a static component.

He shifted once, a fraction.

The hum deepened.

A gauge twitched toward red.

The gray-haired woman's head snapped up. "Still."

Kael froze. Pain lanced through his ankle and up his calf. He wanted to
scream. He didn't, because screaming was movement, and movement was
strain.

He swallowed it down and stared at the iron grating beneath him, where
darkness pooled like ink.

The knowing pressed into him constantly now, not trying to show him
secrets but aligning him to the system's rhythm. It was less like sight
and more like load awareness---a sense of how weight traveled through
braces and chains, where it concentrated, where it threatened to
fracture.

He could feel stress as clearly as he used to feel hunger.

That terrified him more than the pain.

A worker approached during what Kael guessed was a shift change---though
nothing in the chamber signaled it except the arrival of different
faces.

A younger man this time, eyes too bright with nervousness, held out
another cup of water.

"Here," he said, not meeting Kael's gaze.

Kael took it carefully. The chain of the harness clinked, and the hum
responded with a faint change, as if the chamber noticed even that small
sound.

The worker flinched. "Sorry."

Kael swallowed. "How many shifts have passed?"

The worker hesitated. Then he shrugged without commitment. "Enough."

Kael stared at him. "Is there no replacement?"

The worker's mouth tightened. "They said one was coming."

"They," Kael echoed softly.

The knowing surged at the word, tugging upward through the layers of
obligation Kael could now sense like stacked stone. He saw the chain of
command as a structural feature, not a hierarchy: pressure moved
downward; blame moved nowhere.

Kael's head throbbed. He shut the knowing down as best he could.

The worker backed away quickly, as if proximity itself was dangerous.

Kael watched him go and felt something settle in his gut: he was no
longer being treated as a person trapped in a bad position.

He was being treated as a component under load.

The workers did small things for him---water, a strip of cloth pressed
against his bleeding nose, a brief adjustment to the harness so it
didn't cut as deeply into his collarbones. None of it felt like
kindness. It felt like maintenance.

No one apologized.

Apologies implied wrongness.

This was procedure.

At some point---Kael wasn't sure when---his hands began to tremble. Not
from fear. From fatigue. The shake traveled up his arms, into his
shoulders, becoming a subtle vibration that threatened to spread through
the harness.

Kael fought it with breath. With clenched jaw. With the sharp focus of a
man holding a fragile thing over a long drop.

The knowing pressed harder, almost angry at his weakness.

He felt it then, clearly: the system had begun to rely on his presence.
It had adjusted to him the way it adjusted to counterweights. His
steadiness became part of the equilibrium.

If he left suddenly, the chamber would react violently.

If he collapsed, it would react even worse.

He was a living brace.

And living braces fail.

The gray-haired woman returned again, carrying something wrapped in
cloth.

Food.

Hard bread, maybe, and a strip of dried fish. Not much. Enough to keep
his body from shutting down.

Kael's eyes narrowed. "Now you feed me."

She didn't look offended. "We keep the load stable."

Kael swallowed. The bread was dry enough to scrape his throat raw. He
ate anyway, because hunger made shaking worse.

He forced himself to speak between bites. "What happened to the last
one?"

The gray-haired woman didn't answer immediately.

That silence was heavier than any word.

Kael's pulse quickened. "Tell me."

She looked at him then, eyes flat with exhaustion. "He held longer than
he should have."

Kael felt cold spread through his chest. "And?"

"And the replacement was delayed," she said quietly. "Because delay is
cheaper than doing it right."

Kael stared at her, breath shallow. "He died."

The woman's jaw tightened. She didn't deny it.

Kael's stomach lurched. The knowing surged violently, showing him the
missing presence like a dark shape in the system's memory---an absence
that had once been a person.

Kael gagged, fighting bile.

The woman's voice was almost gentle. "Stay still."

Kael laughed---small, broken. "That's what you said to him too, wasn't
it?"

The woman didn't answer.

That silence was confirmation.

Kael closed his eyes and tried to steady his breathing. The chamber's
hum continued, indifferent. Above, counterweights swayed slowly. Below,
water pulled inward

with patient certainty.

Kael understood then, with awful clarity:

They weren't waiting for a replacement.

They were waiting to see how long Kael would hold.

If he held long enough, the system would decide he was efficient.

If he held too long, he would die, and they would replace him with
someone else---another worker, another body, another debt paid in flesh.

Kael opened his eyes.

His gaze fell on the levers at the control station. The chains. The
counterweight drums inset into the walls. The iron braces that
distributed load with brutal precision.

The knowing pulsed, offering him something he didn't want: the map of
the system.

It showed him weak points---places where a small shift would
redistribute load upward rather than down. Places where a gate could be
opened or closed to redirect pressure through a different corridor.
Places where the system could be forced to compensate elsewhere.

It also showed him the cost.

Every reroute meant strain somewhere else. Every shift meant someone
else had to bear weight.

Kael felt his throat tighten. His hands trembled harder.

He could attempt an escape.

But if he simply unhooked himself and ran, the chamber would buck. The
surge would propagate. Workers would die. Maybe hundreds above, if the
mid-tier braces snapped.

He could not leave cleanly.

He would have to choose where the weight went.

Kael stared at the iron grating beneath him, at the darkness where the
water moved wrong. His head pulsed with pain

and structural awareness in equal measure.

He thought of Mirek. Replaced by silence.

He thought of Tessa. Replaced by water.

He thought of Bram. Replaced by distance.

Gravewater replaced what it broke. Always.

Kael swallowed hard.

Then, slowly, he began to plan.

Not how to survive.

How to leave in a way the system couldn't ignore---without turning some
nameless worker into the next missing load.

His mouth tasted like blood.

His body shook.

And the knowing, heavy and relentless, showed him exactly how much
anything would cost.
