# CHAPTER 35

Morning came without mercy.

Not sunlight---Gravewater rarely bothered with that---but motion. The
harbor woke like a wounded thing, restless and defensive. Bells still
rang, though farther apart now, less frantic, more deliberate. The city
had moved past panic. It had entered enforcement.

Kael watched from the loft of the abandoned boathouse, peering through a
warped plank that gave him a narrow view of the canal. His body felt
like it had been taken apart and put back together by someone in a
hurry. Every breath tugged at bruised ribs. His shoulder burned dully.
His ankle had swollen again, stiff and unforgiving.

He did not test it yet.

He let the knowing surface instead.

The harbor was tightening in layers.

Watch boats patrolled in slow sweeps, not chasing, not searching
wildly---filtering. Dockmasters stood with clerks beside them, checking
papers, counting faces, marking lists. Ships that should have been
loading were stalled, crews arguing quietly with men who held seals
instead of weapons.

Gravewater wasn't trying to catch him sprinting for the water.

It was trying to make sure no one left who wasn't supposed to.

Kael closed his eyes and exhaled through his nose.

That meant two things.

First: escape would not be clean.

Second: the city still believed he was human enough to move through
systems.

That was their mistake.

He stayed hidden through the morning, listening to the harbor breathe.
He heard names shouted. Cargo shifted. A fight broke out two piers over
and ended abruptly when Watch stepped in with practiced calm.

The sea itself looked wrong.

Too still.

Too patient.

Kael remembered the rumors from the brothel---ships vanishing, hulls
crushed, lights beneath the water. He didn't feel drawn to the sea so
much as pushed toward it. The city had closed every other door.

By midday, the pattern sharpened.

Certain ships were being delayed repeatedly. Others---older, uglier,
less documented---were waved through with only cursory checks. Not the
merchant lines. Not the guild vessels.

The desperate ones.

Kael focused on those.

A narrow freighter sat low in the water near the outer quay, its hull
scarred and patched. Its name had been scraped and repainted so many
times it barely counted as one anymore. Crew moved fast and quiet,
avoiding attention. No banners. No guild marks.

Kael felt the knowing tug.

That ship wasn't safe.

But it was possible.

He waited until the Watch rotated, then slipped out of the boathouse and
moved with the flow of dock labor---head

down, shoulders hunched, pack hidden beneath a tarp stolen from the
loft. He limped deliberately, leaning into the injury so it looked real
and uninteresting.

No one stopped him.

Not yet.

He passed two checkpoints by staying just close enough to other workers
that he blended into their annoyance and fatigue. When a clerk glanced
his way, Kael let his gaze slide past without meeting it, the way people
who expected nothing from authority always did.

It worked.

Mostly.

Near the outer quay, a Watchman stepped into his path, baton resting
casually against his thigh.

"You," the Watchman said. "Where you headed."

Kael swallowed and kept his voice flat. "Cargo. Pier seven."

The Watchman studied him, eyes flicking to Kael's ankle, the way his
weight favored one side.

"You're hurt."

Kael nodded. "Dock work does that."

A pause.

Too long.

The knowing flared---not danger, but timing.

Kael shifted his weight, just enough.

A shout erupted behind them---someone arguing at a checkpoint, voices
raised. The Watchman glanced back reflexively.

Kael moved.

Five feet sideways, appearing behind a stack of crates, then another
five into the shadow beneath the pier railing. He didn't run. He flowed,
slipping through the blind spots the city's systems couldn't cover
without becoming chaos.

By the time the Watchman turned back, Kael was gone.

Kael reached the freighter as a crate was being hauled aboard. He
grabbed the end without asking, letting the crew curse at him like he
belonged. No one questioned extra hands when everyone was nervous.

The crate went into the hold.

Kael followed it down.

The hold smelled of tar, wet wood, and something coppery that made his
skin prickle. Cargo was stacked haphazardly---barrels, crates, bundles
wrapped in oilcloth. No inventory posted. No neat rows.

Perfect.

Kael slid between two stacks and wedged himself into a narrow space near
the hull, pack pressed tight to his chest. He wrapped himself in shadow
and stillness and waited.

Above him, the ship groaned as lines were cast off.

Voices shouted.

A bell rang.

The freighter lurched.

Kael closed his eyes as the harbor noises began to recede---not vanish,
just blur into distance.

Gravewater was still there.

Watching.

But for the first time in days, it was not closing its fist around his
throat.

The ship creaked and turned toward open water.

Kael stayed hidden, heart hammering, every sense stretched thin. He
didn't relax. He didn't celebrate.

He simply held on as the city that had made him---and tried to break
him---slid slowly out of reach.

This was not freedom.

This was flight.

And the sea, dark and waiting beyond the harbor mouth, did not feel like
salvation.

It felt like the next test.
