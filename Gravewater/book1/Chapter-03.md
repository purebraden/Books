# CHAPTER 3

Gravewater did not care that Mirek was dead.

It didn't slow. It didn't hush. It didn't even shift its rhythms in a
way anyone would call respect. The water kept moving as it always had,
pressing into cracks and pulling away again, leaving damp lines on stone
like punctuation no one read anymore. Platforms creaked under the same
familiar weights. Dockhands shouted the same insults. The Watch made
their same measured rounds, boots striking wet boards with the
confidence of men who believed the city still belonged to them.

Only the crew's timing was wrong.

They met that morning because habit demanded it. Not the full crew. Just
the three of them, standing in a narrow cut between a net shed and a
storage wall where the wind couldn't quite reach. It was one of Mirek's
preferred spots---dry enough, hidden enough, close enough to the lower
channels that you could hear trouble before it found you.

Tessa arrived first and didn't sit.

Kael arrived second and did, because his legs insisted on routine even
when his mind refused it. Bram was already there, leaning in a shadow
that looked like it had been made

for him.

No one spoke for a long time.

The silence didn't feel peaceful. It felt unfinished.

Kael noticed how they stood without Mirek. Tessa's posture filled space
aggressively, as if daring the city to dispute her claim. Bram took less
space than usual, shoulders angled, attention outward rather than
inward. Kael sat and watched them both, his hands resting on his knees
where they couldn't betray him by shaking.

He kept thinking of Mirek's weight in his arms. How wrong it had felt.
How light.

Tessa broke the silence first. "We're short."

Bram nodded once. Kael didn't argue, because there was nothing to argue
with.

"There's a run I had lined up," Tessa continued. "Not worth it now."

Kael glanced up. "Which one?"

"West platform, oil tins and wire. Same kind of thing Mirek liked," she
said, and her voice tightened on the last words. "Requires someone who
can carry and someone who can talk."

Kael felt the smallest sting behind his eyes, sharp and brief. He
breathed through it.

"We don't take it," Kael said.

Tessa's jaw flexed. "We can't afford to start saying no."

"We can't afford to start getting caught," Bram replied.

Tessa shot him a look. Bram didn't flinch.

Kael watched them and realized something he didn't like: Mirek had been
the buffer. The loudness. The part that turned sharp edges into jokes.
Without him, every disagreement landed clean.

"We take smaller work," Kael said. "One-person jobs. Low

risk."

Tessa's laugh was short. "Low risk doesn't feed you."

Kael didn't answer immediately. He was looking past her toward the open
street where a Watch patrol passed in the distance, lanterns swinging.
The pattern was wrong.

They weren't supposed to be here this early.

Kael felt the pressure behind his eyes stir, not pain yet, more like a
fingertip tracing the inside of his skull.

"You notice that?" he asked.

Bram followed his gaze. "Patrol's off."

Tessa glanced once and dismissed it with a shrug that sounded louder
than it should have. "Maybe they changed it."

"They don't change it," Bram said.

Tessa's eyes narrowed. "They do if they want someone to notice."

Kael felt his stomach tighten. That was the kind of sentence you didn't
say unless you already believed the worst.

"We move," Kael said.

They split without ceremony. Kael took the outer route first, not
because it was the safest but because it showed him the most. Gravewater
was built in layers, each level a compromise between what was needed and
what could be held. Most people moved in straight lines when they could.
Kael moved in curves, letting the city reveal itself in fragments: a
broken railing repaired with rope instead of wood, a fresh chalk mark on
a doorframe, a merchant counting coins twice with a hand that shook too
much.

He watched hands. He watched eyes. He watched where people chose to
stand.

He saw someone watching him.

Not openly. Not like a Watchman. Just a man leaning at the

corner of a stairwell, pretending to be interested in a basket of dried
fish. His gaze tracked Kael a moment too long, then dropped away as if
embarrassed.

Kael didn't change pace. He didn't acknowledge the man. He just let the
detail settle.

By the time Kael reached the meeting place near the lift, the pressure
behind his eyes had become a dull ache. He tried to blame it on poor
sleep, on grief, on the fact that his jaw had been clenched for two days
straight.

Tessa was already there, standing near the edge of a platform where the
boards bowed slightly under the weight of too many feet. Bram arrived
shortly after, quiet and sharp.

"You're being followed," Bram said without preamble.

Kael nodded. "I saw one. You?"

"Tessa?" Bram asked.

Tessa's expression didn't change. "Two. Different faces."

Kael exhaled slowly. "So it's not just Watch."

Bram's gaze hardened. "No."

Tessa shifted her stance. "We need to decide what to do with the
ledger."

Kael felt his stomach dip. He'd almost forgotten it existed in the space
between Mirek's death and the city's persistence. Almost.

"It's still hidden," Kael said.

"For now," Tessa replied. "But Mirek's dead. People talk when someone
dies. People ask why."

Bram spoke carefully. "We dump it."

Tessa's eyes flashed. "We don't even know what it's worth."

"We know what it costs," Bram said.

Kael didn't speak right away. He thought of the object under the boards.
Not as paper, not as ink, but as weight. A knot tied into something
larger. He hated that his mind framed it

like that.

He stood. "We move it."

Bram's head tilted. "Where?"

"Not here," Kael said.

Tessa nodded once. "Agreed."

They didn't argue about where to take it in the open. Gravewater had
ears, and the people who listened were rarely the ones you saw. Kael led
them through two levels and across a narrow bridge that rocked gently
over a channel. Tessa kept her hand near her knife. Bram walked with his
hood down for once, letting his face be seen by anyone watching---an old
trick, and not a comforting one. It was a signal: we know you're there.

They reached a small room they used sometimes, a place above a shuttered
chandlery where the air smelled of wax and damp wood. Mirek had
complained about the smell every time they came here. The memory hit
Kael like a sudden cold draft, sharp enough that he had to steady
himself against the wall.

Tessa noticed. Her eyes softened for a fraction of a second, then
hardened again as if she regretted the softness.

Kael pulled up the floorboard and retrieved the wrapped ledger.

It felt heavier than paper should.

Or maybe his hands had changed.

Bram watched him closely. "You all right?"

Kael nodded. "Just tired."

It wasn't a lie, but it wasn't the whole truth either.

They argued in low voices. Not about whether the ledger was
dangerous---that was agreed. They argued about what dangerous meant in
Gravewater.

Tessa wanted to sell it fast, to turn risk into coin before the city
could turn it into blood. Bram wanted it gone entirely, burned, drowned,
erased from their lives.

Kael wanted time.

He didn't say that.

Instead he said, "We don't know who wants it."

Tessa's mouth tightened. "Someone killed Mirek."

Bram's voice stayed flat. "Someone killed Mirek and took his pack."

Kael looked down at the ledger. His fingers pressed lightly on the wrap.

"Then they'll come looking," Kael said.

Silence.

Outside, the city creaked.

Tessa spoke first, careful now. "We should leave."

Kael met her gaze. "Leave where?"

Tessa's eyes flicked away. "Up. Out. Anywhere."

Bram didn't laugh this time. He simply said, "There is no out in
Gravewater. Only other layers."

Kael felt the dull ache behind his eyes tighten again, as if the city
itself agreed.

"We can't leave yet," Kael said.

Tessa stared at him. "Because of the ledger?"

Kael hesitated, and in that hesitation he realized he hadn't answered
honestly.

Because of Mirek. Because of the sense that leaving now would turn his
death into something tidy. Because Kael didn't want tidy. He wanted
accounting.

"We can't leave yet," he repeated, and hated how weak it sounded.

Bram stepped closer. "Then we decide quickly. Because

they're closing in."

Kael nodded. He wrapped the ledger again, tighter this time, and slid it
into a new hiding place---higher, drier, harder to reach. When it was
done, he sat back and listened.

There were footsteps on the stairs outside.

Not loud. Not hurried.

Just present.

Tessa's hand drifted to her knife. Bram moved to the window and looked
down into the street.

Kael didn't move. He felt his pulse in his throat, steady but heavy. The
pressure behind his eyes sharpened, and for a heartbeat he thought the
knowing might break through---might tell him who was on the stairs, why
they were there, what they wanted.

He refused it.

The footsteps paused.

Then they continued past, moving upward, as if the person had changed
their mind.

Or as if they wanted the crew to know they could have stopped.

Bram turned from the window. "They're playing with us."

Tessa's smile was thin and humorless. "Welcome to being important."

Kael looked at the floor where the ledger was hidden and felt the first
real understanding settle in his bones:

Mirek's death had not been an end.

It had been an opening.

And the city had begun to look through it.
