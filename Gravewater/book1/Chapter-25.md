# CHAPTER 25

The offer arrived clean.

That alone made it dangerous.

No sealed pouch left where it could be stolen. No runner lingering too
long. No urgency pressed into the wording. The message came through a
courier Kael had never seen before---a woman with steady hands and a
voice trained not to shake. She delivered it, waited while Kael read it,
and left without trying to watch where he went next.

That was new.

Kael stood in the narrow stairwell of a tannery that no longer tanned
anything and read the paper twice.

The terms were precise. Almost polite.

A retrieval, not an execution.

A time window generous enough to allow planning.

Payment split into thirds---deposit, delivery, confirmation.

An exit clause.

An exit clause.

Kael folded the paper and leaned his head back against the stone wall.

"They're learning," he murmured.

The knowing stirred, not urgently, just attentive. The city was thinking
again, this time with its eyes open.

Kael moved before the offer could settle into him.

He didn't accept.

He didn't refuse.

He watched.

For two days he tracked the edges of the job without touching it. He
visited the streets named in the paper at the wrong times. He watched
who lingered and who didn't. He followed one of the couriers halfway
home and peeled off when the path crossed water.

Nothing lunged at him.

No sudden violence.

No nets.

No watchers clumsy enough to reveal themselves.

That worried him more than a blade ever had.

On the third day, Kael went to the meeting point listed at the bottom of
the page.

It was a counting house that pretended to be a chapel. Quiet. Neutral.
Built so people could enter separately and leave without having to pass
one another. Kael arrived early and stood where the light fell wrong,
letting his presence be noticed without being obvious.

The man who arrived ten minutes later wore gray and

nothing else of note.

No weapon visible. No bodyguard. No nerves.

Kael didn't like him.

"You're not dead," the man said pleasantly.

Kael smiled. "You're disappointed."

"Relieved," the man corrected. "Dead men don't renegotiate."

Kael's smile sharpened. "So you expected me to."

The man inclined his head slightly. "Possibility."

Kael glanced at the building's corners, the exits, the distance between
them. The knowing slid everything into clean alignment.

"You wrote this like a professional," Kael said. "That means you've lost
assets before."

The man didn't deny it. "You cost people money."

Kael shrugged. "They paid me to."

A pause.

Then the man chuckled quietly. "Fair."

He gestured to the bench between them. Kael didn't sit.

"You're not here to kill me," Kael said.

"No."

"You're not here to hire me either."

"Not yet."

Kael crossed his arms. "Then say the real reason."

The man met his eyes. "We need to know if you can be relied upon not to
destabilize everything you touch."

Kael laughed once, sharp. "Then you picked the wrong person."

The man smiled thinly. "You misunderstand. Destabilization is
acceptable. Unpredictability is expensive."

Kael felt the truth of that settle in his bones.

This wasn't a threat.

It was an accounting problem.

"What's the job," Kael asked.

The man slid a thin folder across the bench. "A shipment. Scheduled.
Documented. Protected by process more than steel."

Kael didn't touch it. "And if I refuse."

"Then nothing happens," the man said. "Which would also be useful."

Kael studied him for a long moment.

"Who funds you," Kael asked.

The man's eyes flickered, just slightly.

Kael smiled. "There it is."

Silence stretched.

Finally, the man said, "You're more expensive alive than dead."

Kael nodded. "Good."

He reached out, took the folder, and flipped it open.

Inside were schedules, weights, manifests. A boat name appeared twice.
Then again, on a different page. Always attached to delays. Adjustments.
Fees that didn't line up.

Kael closed the folder.

"This isn't a job," he said. "It's a test."

"Yes."

"And you're watching to see if I steal the deposit and vanish."

The man smiled. "Would you?"

Kael considered it honestly.

"Not today."

The man exhaled. Relief, quickly masked.

Kael leaned forward. "Here are my terms."

The man stiffened. "You don't set---"

Kael cut him off. "I do now."

Silence fell again.

"I take the job," Kael continued, "but I choose the timing. I

choose the approach. And if I find a second ledger attached to this
shipment, I keep it."

The man's jaw tightened. "That's not acceptable."

Kael tilted his head. "Then you can try someone else."

They both knew there wasn't anyone else.

The man weighed it. Kael could almost hear the sums running behind his
eyes.

"Fine," the man said at last. "But if this goes wrong---"

Kael smiled. "It already did. You just don't know how yet."

He stood, tucked the folder under his arm, and turned to leave.

"Kael," the man said, using his name for the first time.

Kael stopped.

"You're not invisible anymore," the man said quietly.

Kael glanced back. "I was never invisible. You just weren't looking."

He left the counting house and stepped back into the city, feeling the
shift immediately. Doors still opened. Fences still dealt. Jobs still
whispered.

But now there was a shape to it.

A current.

And at the center of it was a boat, a shipment, and something big enough
that the city was willing to stop trying to kill him just to see if he'd
take it.

Kael walked on, already mapping exits, already counting distances,
already planning how to turn a test into a theft.

Because the city thought it had finally learned how to deal with him.

And Kael had just learned what he was
