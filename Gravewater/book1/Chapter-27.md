# CHAPTER 27

The job came with margins.

Kael noticed that first---the space built into it. Time windows that
didn't scrape each other raw. Routes that overlapped without tangling.
Payment that didn't need to be rushed because the people behind it
expected the work to take as long as it took.

Margins meant confidence.

Margins also meant more than one hand on the rope.

Kael took the offer anyway.

He didn't acknowledge it directly. He never did. He let it sit where it
had been placed, folded twice, ink clean and dry. He read it, memorized
it, then burned it in a dish meant for food and watched the ash curl
like it had opinions.

Then he went to look at the place.

The annex stood where the mid-tiers leaned toward the water, a building
that had grown outward instead of upward, bolted onto older stone with
newer wood, patched and repatched until it looked like a single thought
from a distance. People passed it every day without noticing. That was
its purpose.

It wasn't guarded like something precious. It was guarded like something
necessary.

Kael watched it for three days.

He learned the rhythm first. Not patrols---those were easy---but pauses.
The moments where men stopped to talk, where

carts were delayed because a signature took longer than it should have,
where a door was left ajar because everyone involved assumed it would be
closed by someone else.

He learned which windows were opened for air and which were opened for
convenience. He learned which lights burned through the night and which
were extinguished as a matter of habit. He learned the sound of the
place when it was empty and the sound it made when it was lying about
being empty.

On the second night, he tested it.

A loose stone here. A shifted crate there. Nothing that would trip
alarms---nothing dramatic. Just enough to see who corrected the error
and how quickly.

The corrections came fast.

That didn't worry him.

What worried him was how quietly they came.

No shouting. No accusations. No scrambling. Just men and women stepping
into place and fixing things like they'd been told to expect it.

Kael didn't back out.

He adjusted.

On the fourth night, he went in.

He didn't climb. He didn't swim. He didn't do anything that would look
clever in hindsight. He walked with purpose through the service corridor
where carts were expected to move late and linger longer than they
should. He timed it so a clerk would be arguing with a driver about a
seal that had been broken the day before.

People who are arguing don't look up.

He slipped through the side door five feet behind a man carrying ledgers
and matched his pace for three steps before

peeling off into a shadow that hadn't been there a moment earlier.

Inside, the annex smelled like paper and oil and old salt. Lamps glowed
behind glass. The floors were swept but never clean. Every sound had a
reason.

Kael moved with the building, not against it. He crossed when carts
crossed. He paused when people paused. He slid sideways when a door
opened in front of him, then forward again when it closed.

He didn't rush.

That was the key.

Rushing was how you told a place you didn't belong.

The first obstacle was a narrow room with desks arranged to funnel
traffic past a single counter. The clerk stationed there had learned to
recognize faces, not intentions. Kael waited until a stack of papers
fell---accidentally, deliberately, it didn't matter---and stepped
through the gap while the clerk bent to retrieve them.

The second obstacle was a man with a badge he didn't understand and a
knife he hadn't used in years. Kael watched the man's posture, waited
for the moment his weight shifted to his bad knee, then crossed behind
him and left him standing there with the sense that something had just
gone wrong without knowing what.

Kael left no bodies behind him.

Not because he couldn't.

Because he didn't need to.

The annex opened into a long hall lined with doors that were never
locked because the locks were the doors themselves---numbers, schedules,
expectations. Kael traced them with his eyes until he found the one that
didn't quite belong.

The hinges were newer. The threshold had been replaced.

Someone had tried to make it invisible by making it better.

Kael slipped inside.

The room was smaller than he expected. No grand vault. No heavy chests.
Just shelves and tables and a single reinforced cabinet set into the
wall, its seams precise enough to suggest more care than strength.

Kael crossed the space and rested his hand on the cabinet.

He felt it immediately.

Not weight. Not resistance.

Significance.

This wasn't valuable because it was rare. It was valuable because it
moved things.

He opened it.

Inside was a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and twine, sealed with a stamp
he'd seen before---not often, but enough to recognize the pattern. The
cloth was marked with dates, not names. Corrections had been made in
careful script, numbers overwritten with numbers that pretended they'd
always been there.

Kael smiled.

He took the bundle and replaced it with something else of equal size and
lesser importance. He didn't rush the substitution. He matched the
placement exactly. He adjusted the cloth until the folds sat the same
way.

Then he closed the cabinet.

He didn't leave immediately.

He read.

Not everything. Just enough.

The bundle was a ledger, but not the kind that listed money. It listed
movement. Goods that passed through the city without belonging to it.
Delays that cost more than bribes. Names

that appeared only once, and then never again.

The boat appeared twice.

That made it a pattern.

Kael wrapped the ledger back up and secured it against his side. He
breathed once, slow and steady, and turned to leave.

That was when the hall shifted.

Not dramatically. Not alarmingly. Just enough that Kael felt it before
he saw it---the way air changes when a door opens behind you that wasn't
supposed to open yet.

Voices murmured.

Steps overlapped where they hadn't before.

Kael moved.

Five feet to the side, then forward, slipping through a space between
bodies that hadn't been there a second earlier. He kept the ledger
tight, his free hand open and ready.

A man glanced up as Kael passed and frowned---not in fear, but in
confusion. Kael reached out and pressed two fingers against the man's
throat, just enough to make him swallow hard and forget what he'd been
about to say.

Kael kept moving.

The exit he'd planned was clear.

That was the second thing that worried him.

He didn't take it.

Instead, he turned down a narrower passage that smelled of old ink and
damp stone, counted three doors, and stepped sideways through a gap that
led into a storage room where nothing important was ever kept.

He waited there, breathing quietly, until the sounds passed.

Then he left the annex the way he'd come in---unremarkable,
unremembered.

Outside, the night felt ordinary.

Kael crossed two bridges and three alleys before he stopped,

and when he did, it was only to rest his ankle and adjust the wrap. Pain
flared, then settled.

He was alive.

The ledger was secure.

The city hadn't reacted.

That was the third thing that worried him.

Kael didn't return to a safehouse. He went somewhere worse---a place
where people were loud and drunk and convinced nothing important ever
happened to them. He sat in a corner with his back to a wall and ordered
food he didn't finish.

No one bothered him.

No one followed.

When he finally moved again, it was to a room he'd rented under a name
that wasn't his. He barred the door, lit a single lamp, and set the
ledger on the table.

He studied it for a long time before opening it fully.

The handwriting changed three times across the pages. The dates
overlapped in ways that made sense only if you knew which schedules lied
by default. He traced the movement of goods from dock to annex to boat
and back again, noting the places where numbers didn't align unless
someone wanted them not to.

This wasn't just theft.

This was coordination.

Kael closed the ledger and wrapped it again.

He slept with it under his hand.

In the morning, a message waited for him.

Not at the door.

On the table.

He frowned, scanned the room, then picked it up.

The paper was blank except for a single line written in the

same careful hand he'd seen before.

Receipt acknowledged. Balance pending.

Kael smiled slowly.

They knew.

Not everything. Not yet.

But enough.

He folded the note and burned it, then packed what he needed and left
the room without looking back. Outside, Gravewater carried on, unaware
that anything had shifted.

But Kael felt it.

The job had gone too well.

And the city, which had once reacted by lashing out, had done something
far worse.

It had absorbed the impact.

Kael disappeared into the streets, ledger hidden, ankle aching, mind
already turning over the implications.

He hadn't lost.

Not yet.

But somewhere behind him, systems were aligning.

And soon---very soon---those margins he'd noticed at the start would
close.
