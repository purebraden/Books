# PROLOGUE

Kael learned hunger before he learned numbers.

It arrived first as a sound---a thin ringing behind the eyes that made
the world feel too loud and too close. Voices scraped. Light pressed.
Even stillness felt heavy, like something waiting to fall. Later, people
would tell him hunger lived in the belly. They were wrong. Hunger lived
higher than that. It lived where decisions were made.

The city taught him this without words.

Gravewater was always damp, even when it hadn't rained. Stone held the
memory of water the way wood held rot. Lines had been carved into the
lower walls of buildings---some deliberate, some accidental---marking
where the water had reached in years past. No one bothered to erase
them. They weren't warnings. They were records.

Kael was eight when he noticed that the marks never went down.

At that age, he slept where he could: under stairs when they were dry,
in net sheds when the fishermen forgot to lock them, in a narrow space
between two leaning buildings

where the wind didn't quite reach. He learned quickly which places
flooded first and which smells meant you should leave even if it meant
walking all night.

He also learned which adults looked through you and which ones looked at
you.

The woman with the bread stall was the latter.

She worked the morning corner near the lift platform, close enough to
the docks that sailors passed by but far enough that they weren't her
problem anymore. Her hands shook when she stacked loaves. Not
badly---just enough to notice if you paid attention. Kael paid attention
to hands. Hands told you what a person could still afford to lose.

He watched her for three days before he acted.

On the fourth morning, the ringing behind his eyes grew sharp enough
that it felt like pressure. He stood there longer than he meant to,
staring at the steam curling from the fresh bread, counting breaths the
way he did when he needed to slow himself down.

She turned to argue with a supplier.

Kael reached out and took a loaf.

It was warm through the thin cloth of his shirt, heavier than he
expected. He didn't run right away. He walked---fast, but not
panicked---until the corner was out of sight. Only then did he run, feet
slipping on wet stone, heart hammering in his ears.

He didn't stop until the city opened up beneath him, the lower channels
spreading out like dark veins. He crouched behind a broken railing and
waited, listening for shouts.

None came.

His hands shook as he tore the loaf in half. He ate slowly, carefully,
the way he'd learned to eat anything that might be

the last thing you found that day. The warmth eased the ringing, just a
little.

That was when the older boy spoke.

"You going to finish that?"

Kael nearly dropped the bread.

The boy sat on the railing a few steps away, legs dangling over the
drop, posture loose in a way that meant he'd already decided where the
edges were. He didn't look angry. He didn't look impressed. He looked
curious, which was worse.

Kael shook his head and held out the smaller piece.

The boy took it, ate it in three bites, and wiped his hands on his
trousers. "You took from the tired one."

Kael said nothing.

The boy nodded, as if this confirmed something he'd suspected. "They
notice," he said. "Even when they don't chase you."

"What should I take?" Kael asked.

The boy smiled, thin and sharp. "From people who can afford to be
angry."

Then he jumped down and walked away, leaving Kael alone with the last of
the bread and a thought that would not settle.

Kael watched the water for a long time after that, listening to it slide
past the pylons. The city creaked above him, wood shifting against
stone, always adjusting. He understood, dimly, that the bread hadn't
been free. Someone had paid for it. Just not him.

That was the lesson Gravewater repeated in different forms as he grew.

He learned that some doors opened quietly and some announced themselves
no matter how careful you were. He learned that repairs were never meant
to last, only to hold long enough for something else to break first. He
learned

that people spoke about the future only in short distances---until the
next tide, until the next platform adjustment, until the next
inspection.

He learned to watch.

By ten, he could tell which planks would creak before he stepped on
them. By twelve, he knew which Watch patrols followed patterns and which
enjoyed deviation. By fourteen, he understood that most arguments
weren't about truth but about where the cost would land when things went
wrong.

He noticed when a deal felt heavy even before anyone spoke. He noticed
when a smile meant relief and when it meant relief had already been
spent.

He did not have a name for this habit. He only knew that listening kept
him fed.

On nights when the wind came in off the water just right, carrying the
smell of salt and old metal, Kael would wake with the ringing behind his
eyes and sit up, waiting for it to pass. He would think of the bread
stall, of the lines on the walls, of the way the water never quite
stayed where it belonged.

Gravewater did not teach kindness.

It taught accounting.

And Kael learned early that if you did not choose where the weight fell,
the city would choose for you.
