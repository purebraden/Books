# Chapter 8 — Nothing in the Pockets

Kael didn’t plan to test anything.

If he had, it wouldn’t have happened the way it did.

He was moving through the middle quay just after midday, when the docks were loud enough to swallow mistakes.

Nets were being hauled in again after a quiet morning, and the air had that sharp, briny edge that came with a rising tide.

Kael kept his hood down and his hands visible, doing nothing more suspicious than walking where he had a right to walk.

He felt steady.

Heavy, in a way that had nothing to do with exhaustion.

The sense that had settled into him after the alley hadn’t faded.

It had organized itself.

When he looked at people now, things lined up—intentions, habits, risk.

It wasn’t comforting.

It was efficient.

That efficiency was what got him into trouble.

The mark was easy.

Too easy.

A sailor with coin to burn and attention already spent on a dice game near the rope walk.

Kael brushed past, fingers dipping automatically toward the inside pocket— —and closed on nothing.

For a heartbeat, his mind rejected it.

The motion had been clean.

The angle right.

He felt the object leave the mark.

He should have felt leather.

He didn’t.

His fingers came up empty.

Kael’s step faltered.

That was enough.

A hand closed around his arm, hard. “Hold,” a voice said, too close to his ear.

Kael turned, already calculating angles, exits, apologies.

A guard stood behind him, face flat, grip iron.

Another moved in from the side.

Not Watch in force—port security.

Dock enforcers.

The kind who didn’t wear full uniform but carried authority in their posture. “Problem?” Kael asked, keeping his voice even. “Maybe,” the guard said. “You looked like you lost something.” Kael didn’t answer.

He didn’t trust his mouth yet.

The second guard stepped closer, eyes flicking to Kael’s hands, his sleeves, his belt. “Empty your pockets.” Kael hesitated half a breath too long.

That was enough too.

They turned him, pushed him back against a piling where half the quay could see.

Not rough, but thorough.

Professional.

The first guard patted him down briskly, hands sliding over coat, waist, legs.

The second watched Kael’s face, not his body. “Arms up.” Kael lifted them.

The guard checked his sleeves, tugged at the lining, shook out the cuffs.

He pulled Kael’s hood back and ran fingers through his hair, quick and impersonal.

Checked boots.

Checked belt.

Checked the small pouch Kael carried for coin.

Empty.

The second guard took the pouch, turned it inside out, shook it once for show.

Nothing fell.

The guards exchanged a look. “Where is it?” the first one asked.

Kael swallowed. “Where’s what?” The guard leaned in, breath smelling of salt and old tea. “Don’t play clever.” “I don’t have anything,” Kael said.

And for the first time, it was true in a way he didn’t yet understand.

They searched him again.

Slower this time.

More invasive.

Hands pressing harder, fingers probing seams, checking places that stung and places that humiliated.

The quay had gone quiet around them, curiosity pulling eyes their way.

Still nothing.

The guards stepped back.

The second one frowned. “You sure it was him?” The first guard looked Kael over, measuring him anew.

Not guilty now.

Interesting. “Move along,” the guard said finally. “And keep your hands where people can see them.” Kael nodded once and walked away without hurrying.

He didn’t breathe properly until he was three alleys away.

He ducked into a narrow cut between warehouses and pressed his back against the stone, heart hammering now that it was safe to do so.

His hands shook as he looked down at them.

Slowly, deliberately, he reached into the pocket he had aimed for earlier.

Nothing.

Cold spread through his chest.

He reached into another pocket.

Then another.

His pouch.

His sleeves.

Empty.

Kael closed his eyes.

No, he thought.

That’s not possible.

He reached again, the way he would in the dark, guided by habit rather than sight.

His fingers brushed something.

Not cloth.

Not leather.

Space.

It was there and not there at once, like reaching into a shadow that had depth.

His hand closed around something solid, familiar.

Kael pulled.

The coin spilled into his palm, bright and unmistakably real.

Kael stared at it.

Then he laughed once, sharp and breathless, and immediately clamped a hand over his mouth.

He didn’t try again.

Not there.

Not now.

He pushed the coin back the same way it had come and felt it slip out of the world with a sensation like closing a door that had never been open. “Right,” he whispered. “That’s… right.” He stood there for a long moment, letting the tremor pass.

No pain.

No echo.

Just the certainty that something fundamental had changed, and that he had nearly paid for discovering it in the worst possible way.

By dusk, he needed answers.

Not explanations—he knew better than to look for those—but boundaries.

What worked.

What didn’t.

He went to the fence.

The shop didn’t look like much.

A narrow storefront wedged between a cooper and a sailmaker, its windows dusty, its door perpetually half-open.

Inside, the air smelled of oil and old metal.

The man behind the counter looked up as Kael entered, eyes already assessing. “Thought you were dead,” the fence said mildly. “Thought you were honest,” Kael replied.

The fence snorted. “Nobody honest lasts.” Kael laid a small bundle on the counter.

He hadn’t checked what it was yet.

He didn’t need to.

The fence untied the cloth and frowned. “Where’d you get this?” he asked.

Kael hesitated.

Then answered honestly. “Off a job that went bad.” The fence’s eyes flicked up. “How bad?” “Enough,” Kael said.

The fence picked up one of the pieces—a small metal clasp, worked with a pattern Kael recognized instantly. “Low tide make,” Kael said without thinking. “Harbor work.

Not old.

Not new.

Worth more to the right buyer than to you.” The fence froze.

He looked at Kael again, slower this time. “You want to explain how you know that?” Kael shrugged. “Good eye.” The fence snorted again, but there was no humor in it.

He turned the clasp over, then picked up another piece. “And this?” “Anchor-weight balancing pin,” Kael said. “Modified.

Whoever used it didn’t want the ship to sit right.” The fence set it down carefully. “You didn’t open these,” he said. “I didn’t need to.” Silence stretched.

Finally, the fence sighed and reached for his scale. “You’re not wrong,” he said. “I don’t like that.” “But you’ll deal,” Kael said.

The fence smiled thinly. “Because you’re right.” They settled on a price without haggling.

That bothered Kael almost as much as everything else.

When he left, the fence watched him go with an expression that held both interest and caution.

Kael stepped back into the evening air and let it fill his lungs.

Inventory.

Identification.

Abilities he didn’t have names for and didn’t need.

He moved through the crowd differently now—not bolder, not careless—but aware of how much space he took up.

When he reached for something, it was there.

When he looked at something, he understood it.

The city didn’t notice the change.

Not yet.

But somewhere in Gravewater, someone would eventually realize that the wrong person had stopped being empty-handed.

And when they did, Kael knew, the city would adjust again.

He pulled his hood up and disappeared into the crowd, pockets light and full all at once.
