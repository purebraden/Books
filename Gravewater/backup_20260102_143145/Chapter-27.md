# Chapter 27 — The One That Pays Too Clean

Kael didn’t go back to the brothel after the knock.

That room was finished.

Not burned, not watched—marked.

The city had learned one of his habits, and habits were liabilities.

He spent the rest of the night moving between docks, keeping just ahead of attention, letting the harbor noise blur him into another tired shape with nowhere particular to be.

By dawn, the water was louder than the streets.

Ships came and went without rhythm.

Crews changed hands faster than coin.

Men slept on coils of rope instead of beds and called it rest.

Kael watched them the way he watched alleys—looking for what didn’t belong.

That was when the man found him.

Not by surprise.

By patience.

The man leaned against the quay rail beside him as if they’d been standing there together all morning.

He didn’t crowd Kael.

Didn’t speak until the gulls screamed overhead and the moment passed. “No names,” the man said. “No follow-ups.” Kael didn’t look at him. “Then don’t start a conversation.” The man smiled faintly. “You’re still here.” That earned him a glance.

The man was older than he pretended.

Salt in his beard.

Coat worn thin in places that suggested time, not poverty.

He smelled like rope and pitch, not ink or perfume. “I was told you don’t like the Watch,” the man continued. “Or ledgers.

Or being owned.” Kael turned back to the water. “You were told a lot of things.” “I was told one that matters,” the man said. “That when the pay is wrong, you walk.

When it’s too right, you listen.” He set a pouch on the rail between them.

Didn’t open it.

Didn’t shake it.

Just let the weight exist.

Kael picked it up.

Heavy.

Honest coin.

No shaved edges. “That’s too much,” Kael said. “Yes,” the man agreed. “That’s how you know it’s dangerous.” Kael rolled the pouch once in his palm. “What’s the job.” The man hesitated.

Not long.

Just enough. “There’s a hold beneath the old foundations,” he said. “Something stored there that shouldn’t be.” The knowing tightened—not screaming, not warning.

Pulling inward. “What do you want me to do,” Kael asked, “that you won’t send your own people for?” The man exhaled slowly. “Confirm it’s still there.” “That’s a lie,” Kael said calmly.

The man nodded. “Confirm what it is.” Kael handed the pouch back.

The man didn’t take it. “Second half is triple,” he said. “Paid before you leave.” Kael’s jaw tightened. “Who’s paying,” Kael asked.

The man didn’t answer immediately.

He glanced toward the water—toward ships that loaded too fast, toward routes that didn’t like witnesses. “Someone who will notice if you don’t,” he said.

Kael closed his hand around the pouch again.

That was the answer.

They stood in silence as a cutter slipped its lines early, sails catching wind before anyone called the order. “Where’s the entrance,” Kael asked.

The man tilted his chin toward a stretch of stone that never quite dried. “There’s a stair that never floods,” he said. “That alone should worry you.” Kael nodded once. “And after?” he asked.

The man shrugged. “After is yours.” Kael turned away before the man could say anything else.

He didn’t go back to the brothel.

He walked the docks instead, counting lanterns, watching which ships hired loudly and which waited.

He memorized the stretch of stone the man had indicated and the way the water moved around it.

Too calm.

Too patient.

By the time the sun climbed high enough to burn the fog off the water, Kael had already decided.

He didn’t like the job.

He didn’t trust the pay.

And that meant it was already his.
