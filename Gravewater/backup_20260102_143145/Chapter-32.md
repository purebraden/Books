# Chapter 32 — The Accounting Comes Due

The city stopped pretending the problem would stay underground.

It began with closures that didn’t have reasons.

A pier shut for “inspection.” A warehouse reassigned without notice.

Crews dismissed early and told not to ask why.

When people asked anyway, they were paid to forget the question.

Kael watched it happen from the margins.

The stair that never flooded was no longer guarded openly.

The men were gone by morning, replaced not with Watch or muscle but with absence.

That was worse.

The knowing pressed hard near the stone, not pulling now but repelling, like a wound that didn’t want to be touched.

They’d changed the plan.

He didn’t approach the stair again.

He didn’t need to.

The harbor told the story if you listened to what wasn’t being said.

The prepared ship—the quiet one—was being loaded at night now.

No manifests posted.

No shouted orders.

Cargo moved under tarps and down into the hold with ritual care, men stepping where they were told and nowhere else.

Not cargo.

Counterweight.

Kael felt the conclusion settle.

They were going to move it.

Not solve it.

Not destroy it.

Export it.

That explained everything: the sudden need for reinforced hulls, the rush to hire crew who wouldn’t ask questions, the rumors of monsters at sea.

If the thing below wanted motion, water was the cheapest place to give it room.

And if it went wrong out there— —no one would blame the city.

Kael slipped closer, threading between workers who were too focused on where they put their feet to notice anyone else’s.

He caught a whispered exchange between two men hauling a crate too carefully for its size. “…doesn’t like the turn—” “…don’t say that—” “…just move—” The crate shifted.

Every man around it flinched.

Kael backed away before the knowing could sharpen into panic.

This was the end of the job.

Once the ship sailed, the people who had arranged this would erase loose ends.

Witnesses.

Variables.

Anyone who knew too much or had been too close.

That included him.

He moved fast now—not running, not hiding.

Repositioning.

He shed routes he’d used before, abandoned safe angles, crossed spaces he’d avoided because avoidance had taught people where not to look.

By nightfall, he was certain.

They were hunting him.

Not broadly.

Specifically.

A pair of watchers split from a crowd when he crossed the upper quay.

Not muscle.

Not Watch.

Professionals who didn’t rush and didn’t lose him, only adjusted when he adjusted.

Kael let them follow.

He led them through narrowing streets and broken light, testing their discipline, counting how often they hesitated at corners.

They weren’t here to grab him in public.

They were steering him.

Toward the docks.

Toward the prepared ship.

Toward a place where disappearance could be explained.

Kael broke contact deliberately—sideways through a press of bodies, then up and over a low roof before they could decide whether to pursue vertically or horizontally.

He didn’t go far.

He watched.

They regrouped exactly where he expected.

Confirmation.

The job was done.

The cleanup had begun.

Kael settled into stillness on a rooftop overlooking the harbor, eyes fixed on the ship that waited with its weight carefully balanced and its crew carefully chosen.

They weren’t just moving a problem.

They were setting a trap big enough to swallow him in the same motion.

Kael smiled thinly.

Good.

That meant when it broke, it would break loud.
