# Chapter 4 — What the Water Takes

Gravewater did not react to Bram’s death the way Kael expected.

There was no tightening of patrols.

No shouted orders.

No sweep of Lowwake.

The Watch did what it always did when violence threatened to become inconvenient: it reduced the event until it fit inside a ledger.

By midmorning, Bram’s body was gone.

The place where he’d been left—too open to ignore, too deliberate to mistake—was scrubbed clean with salt water and rags that would be burned later.

A Watch notice appeared on a post nearby, nailed up crooked.

Dispute between criminal elements.

Avoid the area after dark.

Kael read it once, then again.

The words meant nothing.

They were meant to end questions, not answer them.

He stood across the street from the post for a long time, hood up, hands in his sleeves, watching people read it and move on.

Some crossed the street.

Some laughed.

Most didn’t slow.

Lowwake absorbed the loss the way it absorbed everything else: by stepping around it.

Tessa didn’t come out that morning.

Kael waited near the cooper’s shop until the sun climbed high enough to warm the stone.

When she still hadn’t appeared, he went to the loft.

The door was shut.

The shutters closed.

He knocked once.

Then again.

No answer.

Kael opened the door slowly.

The room was as they’d left it.

Bram’s pallet was empty.

His few belongings—knife, spare shirt, a chipped mug—were gone.

Not stolen.

Collected.

Tessa sat on her pallet with her knees drawn up, staring at the wall. “They killed him,” she said without looking at Kael. “Yes,” Kael said. “He thought he could fix it,” she said. “He thought he could talk.” Kael didn’t correct her.

There was nothing to correct.

Bram had always believed in leverage he couldn’t see. “They wanted us to know,” Tessa said. “That’s why they left him like that.” “Yes,” Kael said again.

She laughed once, sharp and humorless. “So now what?” Kael didn’t answer right away.

Outside, the harbor creaked.

Somewhere, a bell rang twice. “We stop,” Kael said finally. “We lay low.

We don’t work for a while.” Tessa turned to him then, eyes bright with anger. “That’s what we’ve been doing.” “We stop completely,” Kael said. “We disappear.” “And eat what?” she demanded.

Kael met her gaze. “Less.” That was the moment she looked away.

They didn’t argue.

There was nothing left to argue with.

The city had spoken clearly enough.

They were expendable.

Useful only when quiet.

Disposable when inconvenient.

Kael spent the rest of the day walking Lowwake.

Not working.

Watching.

He noticed things he would’ve ignored before.

A basement door chained shut where it hadn’t been yesterday.

A row of houses with chalk marks on the lintels—not warding, just warning.

Children playing closer to home, their games quieter.

Near dusk, he saw the first barricade.

It was crude.

Broken furniture stacked across an alley mouth, tied together with rope and wire.

No guard.

No sign.

Just a message written in effort.

Don’t go this way.

Kael turned back.

That night, the water moved wrong.

He woke to the sound of something scraping along stone.

Not loud.

Not steady.

The kind of sound you only noticed when it stopped.

Kael lay still, counting breaths, listening.

Nothing else followed.

No shouts.

No running feet.

In the morning, someone was missing.

A woman who sold lamp oil from a pushcart near the old foundations.

Her cart was still there.

Her oil gone cold.

The Watch arrived late.

Looked around.

Took notes. “Fell in,” one guard said to no one in particular.

No one argued.

By the third day, Lowwake had changed its routes.

People took longer paths.

Avoided certain corners.

Left offerings at shrines they didn’t believe in.

By the fourth day, Kael heard a new word.

Not spoken loudly.

Not spoken often.

Slip.

He heard it whispered between two dockhands near the rope walk. “Don’t go down there,” one said. “It slipped again last night.” “What slipped?” The other shrugged. “Someone.” Kael followed the sound.

The place they pointed to was a cluster of buildings near the old foundations, where stone from earlier ages jutted up at odd angles.

The ground there was always damp.

Basements flooded with every high tide.

The Watch hadn’t patrolled it in years.

A chalk mark had been drawn on the nearest wall.

Three lines, slashed downward.

Tessa found him there later, face pale. “I saw it,” she said.

Kael didn’t ask what. “It wasn’t—” She stopped, swallowed. “It wasn’t solid.

But it wasn’t nothing.” “Where?” Kael asked. “Near the flood cellars.

I saw it in the water first.

Then in the wall.” Kael felt a familiar tightening in his chest.

Not fear.

Calculation. “What did it do?” he asked.

Tessa shook her head. “It watched.” They didn’t sleep in the loft that night.

They stayed with a family Tessa knew, packed into a room that smelled of damp cloth and old smoke.

No one spoke after dark.

When water dripped somewhere in the building, every head turned.

Near midnight, a scream cut off too fast.

No one moved.

Kael lay awake, staring at the ceiling, listening to the city choose silence.

By morning, the Watch had cordoned off two blocks.

Not with guards.

With notices.

Unsafe structures.

Flood damage.

Do not enter.

No mention of the missing.

No mention of death.

Kael stood at the edge of the cordon with Tessa and watched people adjust their paths. “They’re letting it feed,” Tessa said quietly.

Kael didn’t answer.

He was already thinking ahead, mapping the routes, the blind spots, the places where the water touched stone and stone touched people.

Whatever was moving in Lowwake wasn’t their fault.

But it was going to make them choose.

And Kael was running out of places to stand.
