# Chapter 2 — Weight of Water

The docks remembered faces better than names.

Kael learned that on his second pass through the lower quay that morning.

Men who hadn’t spared him a glance the day before looked twice now—not because they recognized him, but because something in the air had shifted.

The docks did that sometimes.

Pressure built without sound, like weather.

He kept his pace steady and his hands visible.

Mirek was already working the crowd near the net menders, loud as ever, but his voice carried an edge Kael hadn’t heard before.

Not fear.

Irritation.

The kind that came from feeling watched and not knowing why.

Tessa caught Kael’s eye from across the quay and tilted her head once.

Something’s off.

Kael nodded and drifted wide.

They didn’t need words.

If the docks were tightening, the worst thing they could do was cluster.

Bram wasn’t where he should’ve been.

That bothered Kael more than it should have.

He finished a slow circuit of the quay without lifting a single coin.

That bothered him too.

Easy marks were there—sailors fresh off night work, a factor’s runner distracted by an argument—but the rhythm was wrong.

Every time Kael stepped into position, someone shifted.

Not deliberately.

Just enough.

By midday, the Watch appeared.

Not in force.

Not openly.

Two guards near the weigh house, another by the salt road.

Casual.

Hands resting on belts.

Not looking for anyone in particular.

Looking for something.

Kael cut back toward Lowwake and found Bram waiting near the cooper’s shop, leaning against the wall with his arms folded. “You’re late,” Kael said.

Bram shrugged. “Had to take the long way.” “Why?” Bram didn’t answer right away.

He scratched at his jaw and glanced down the alley. “Word’s moving,” he said finally. “About a paper.” Kael felt the weight under his sleeve settle heavier. “Whose word?” he asked.

Bram shook his head. “Doesn’t matter.

It’s not about us.

Not yet.

But someone noticed something go missing that wasn’t coin.” Mirek joined them a moment later, face flushed, grin gone. “Watch’s thicker than it should be,” he said. “Not stopping anyone.

Just standing wrong.” Tessa came last, eyes sharp, jaw tight. “They’re asking questions,” she said. “Quiet ones.” That settled it.

They moved to the sleeping loft and shut the shutters tight.

The room felt smaller than it had the night before.

Bram sat on his pallet.

Mirek paced.

Tessa leaned against the wall with her arms crossed.

Kael pulled the paper from his sleeve and laid it flat between them.

They stared. “Tide schedule,” Mirek said. “So?” “Names,” Tessa said. “Look closer.” Bram leaned forward. “Those aren’t cargo notes.” “No,” Kael said. “They’re people.” Silence stretched. “Someone’s running something through Deep Reach,” Bram said slowly. “Something that needs timing.” “And doesn’t want eyes on it,” Kael said.

Mirek swore. “Then why keep it on paper?” “Because paper doesn’t forget,” Tessa said. “And someone wanted it remembered.” They didn’t say what that meant.

They didn’t need to.

Kael folded the paper again and slid it away. “We don’t touch this,” Mirek said quickly. “We put it back.

Quiet.

Before someone decides to make noise.” “Put it back where?” Bram asked. “You don’t know where it came from.” “That’s the point,” Mirek snapped.

Kael shook his head. “Too late.” They all looked at him. “If it was meant to stay hidden,” Kael said, “it wouldn’t have been carried through the quay.

Someone was sloppy or someone wanted it moved.

Either way, returning it just tells them we saw it.” “So what?” Mirek asked. “We burn it?” “No,” Kael said. “We wait.” Waiting wasn’t easy.

The next two days passed slow and tense.

They worked smaller marks, safer ones, and split early.

The Watch stayed visible but uninterested.

No one was stopped.

No one was searched.

That was worse.

On the third night, a boy from Lowwake went missing.

Then a dockhand.

People whispered about drink and falling into the water, but no one found bodies.

The harbor swallowed a lot of things, but it usually left something behind.

Tessa stopped sleeping through the night.

Kael found her on the roof one evening, watching the tide creep in. “Something’s moving wrong,” she said without looking at him. “In the water?” he asked.

She shook her head. “In the streets.

The slums near the old foundations.

People are avoiding places they don’t usually avoid.” “Because of the Watch?” “No,” she said. “Because the water’s listening.” Kael didn’t answer.

He didn’t know what to say to that.

The next night, Mirek didn’t come back.

They found him at dawn, crumpled near the old net sheds.

His head was turned wrong.

No blood.

No obvious wound.

Just a broken neck from a fall that hadn’t needed to be fatal.

The Watch called it an accident.

Kael stood with Bram and Tessa at the edge of the crowd and said nothing.

That was the moment the docks stopped pretending.

By nightfall, whispers were everywhere.

About things in the slums.

About water moving where it shouldn’t.

About shapes seen in reflection that weren’t there when you turned.

The Watch posted notices.

Unsafe structures.

Flood damage.

Avoid after dark.

No mention of Mirek.

Bram drank too much.

Tessa watched too closely.

Kael did what he always did.

He planned.

By the end of the week, they knew where the pattern was tightening.

Lowwake’s lowest blocks.

Places with flooded basements and rotting supports.

Places no one would miss quickly.

And they knew something else.

Whatever was happening wasn’t tied to them.

Not yet.

That didn’t make it safer.

It made it closer.
