# Chapter 10 — An Offer That Doesn’t Ask

The job came to Kael without using his name.

That alone told him more than the words ever could.

He was cutting through the upper edge of Lowwake just after dusk—far enough from the docks to avoid the noise, close enough to feel the tide through the stone—when a boy fell into step beside him.

Young, but not new.

The way he walked said he knew which streets forgave mistakes and which didn’t. “You walk fast,” the boy said, breathless but smiling.

Kael didn’t slow. “You talk too much.” The boy grinned wider. “That’s why they sent me.” Kael stopped.

The boy almost ran into him. “Say it,” Kael said.

The boy swallowed and glanced around, then lowered his voice. “There’s a man who wants something moved.

Quiet.

Clean.

He says you’re good at that.” “People say a lot of things,” Kael replied. “He says you don’t ask questions,” the boy added quickly. “And that you don’t stay where the work happens.” That was closer.

Kael studied the boy properly now.

Clean enough.

Shoes worn thin in a way that suggested errands, not running.

No knife visible, but that didn’t mean much.

The boy’s eyes kept flicking to corners, doorways.

He was scared—but not of Kael. “When?” Kael asked. “Now,” the boy said. “Before the tide turns.” Kael exhaled slowly. “Where?” The boy smiled with relief. “Deep Reach.” Kael almost laughed.

He didn’t.

They didn’t go straight there.

That would’ve been too obvious.

Instead, the boy led him through a winding path that climbed away from the water, then curved back down through a series of narrow streets where the buildings leaned together like conspirators.

Kael counted turns without thinking, marking distances, noting blind spots.

Fifteen feet between doorways here.

Ten-foot stretch with no cover there.

An alley barely wide enough for two people to pass without touching.

The city wrote itself in squares now.

They stopped at a warehouse that didn’t look protected until you knew how to look.

No guards in sight.

No lanterns.

But the stones around the door were worn smooth from traffic, and the locks were newer than the wood they held.

The boy knocked once.

Then twice.

Then waited.

The door opened just wide enough to admit them.

Inside, the warehouse smelled of oil and rope and something faintly sweet Kael couldn’t place.

Crates were stacked high along the walls, leaving a clear space in the center where a table had been set up with a single lantern.

Three people waited there.

Two were muscle.

That was obvious.

Not dockhands—too still, too balanced.

Knives at their belts, hands relaxed but ready.

The third sat at the table.

Not the man from the docks.

Someone else.

Older.

Better dressed.

No visible weapon.

His posture radiated the kind of calm that came from knowing violence would happen for him if needed. “Kael,” the man said.

Kael didn’t correct him. “That’s not my name,” Kael replied.

The man smiled. “It is tonight.” Kael felt the resistance immediately.

Not a lie.

A decision. “You sent a boy,” Kael said. “That’s inefficient.” “I wanted to see if you’d come,” the man said. “And if you’d bring anything with you.” Kael didn’t move. “You didn’t,” the man continued. “Good.” “Say the job,” Kael said.

The man gestured to a crate near the wall.

Smaller than the others.

Reinforced at the corners.

Counted. “Something went missing,” the man said. “Not stolen.

Misplaced.

I need it recovered before it becomes a problem.” Kael’s head throbbed faintly.

He didn’t look at the crate. “Why me?” he asked.

The man folded his hands. “Because if my people handle it, someone dies.

If the Watch handles it, many people die.

If you handle it—” “Someone still dies,” Kael finished.

The man tilted his head. “Possibly.” Kael considered the room.

The distances.

The exits.

The way the muscle shifted subtly when he moved his weight. “This is a test,” Kael said.

The man smiled again. “Everything is.” “What happens if I say no?” Kael asked.

The man didn’t answer right away.

Instead, he nodded to one of the guards.

The man stepped aside, revealing a shape in the shadows Kael hadn’t noticed at first.

A chalk mark on the wall.

Three slashes.

Lowwake’s warning. “You go back to small things,” the man said gently. “And the city keeps adjusting around you.

Eventually, it adjusts you out.” Kael closed his eyes briefly.

When he opened them, he nodded once. “Show me where it was last seen.” The man’s smile faded just a touch. “You’re efficient.” “Don’t mistake that for agreement,” Kael said.

The job took him into a part of the Deep Reach that never fully dried.

Old stone.

Narrow passages.

Water seeping through cracks that shouldn’t have led anywhere.

Kael moved alone.

He found signs easily.

Too easily.

A disturbed reflection.

Scuffed stone.

A place where something had been dragged—not heavy, but unwilling.

The trail led into a storage chamber half-flooded at high tide.

Kael stopped at the threshold.

The knowing pressed in, hard enough to make him grit his teeth.

Something was inside.

Not the Tide-Slipper.

Something smaller.

Meaner.

Caged.

Kael stepped in.

The thing lunged.

It was fast, but Kael was faster.

He didn’t think.

He moved.

Ten feet to the side as the creature struck where he had been, blade flashing in a tight arc.

The hit landed clean, deeper than it should have.

The creature screamed—a thin, reedy sound that echoed off stone.

Kael felt the pull then.

The narrowing.

The focus.

He ended it.

The world lurched.

For an instant, Kael was somewhere else—fifteen feet away, pressed against the far wall, breath ragged, heart hammering.

The creature’s body hit the ground where he had been standing, lifeless.

Kael stared at the space between. “Right,” he whispered. “That’s… new.” He retrieved the missing item—a small, sealed coffer—and left without lingering.

When he returned to the warehouse, the man accepted the coffer without opening it. “Well?” the man asked.

Kael wiped his blade on a rag. “It won’t be a problem again.” The man nodded. “Good.” “And now?” Kael asked.

The man leaned back. “Now you decide whether you’re going to keep solving problems like this.” Kael met his gaze. “I already did.” He turned and walked out before the man could answer.

Outside, the tide had turned.

Water moved back toward the sea, carrying with it things the city didn’t want to keep.

Kael blended into the night, mind racing, muscles aching.

He had survived the test.

That didn’t mean he’d passed.

It meant the real work had begun.
