# Chapter 25 — The Room You Don’t Ask About

Kael didn’t choose the brothel because it was comfortable.

He chose it because no one looked closely there.

The building leaned inward on itself, three stories of stained plaster and tired wood pressed between a chandlery and a boarding house that had stopped advertising vacancies years ago.

Music drifted from the lower floor at all hours—never loud enough to be cheerful, never quiet enough to be suspicious.

He entered just before dusk, moving with the easy certainty of someone who knew where he was going without caring who noticed.

No one stopped him.

That mattered.

The woman at the door didn’t ask his name.

She took his coin, glanced at his face once—just long enough to register he wasn’t trouble for her—and nodded toward the stairs. “Second floor,” she said. “Last door.

Don’t bleed on the sheets.” “I’ll do my best,” Kael replied.

The room was small, clean enough, and wrong in all the right ways.

One window, barred but loose.

A wardrobe that had been repaired too often to hide anything properly.

A narrow bed with a mattress that knew too many secrets to complain.

Kael locked the door, then didn’t trust it.

He moved the wardrobe just enough to create resistance.

Not a barricade—an excuse.

If someone came through the door, they’d have to work for it.

That gave him time.

He sat on the bed and listened.

The brothel breathed differently than the rest of the city.

People talked too much.

Laughed too easily.

Lied without effort.

The knowing drifted through the walls in fragments—desire, fear, negotiation—but none of it stuck to him.

He was a shadow among indulgences.

After an hour, Kael moved to the window and loosened the bar another inch.

He leaned out just far enough to see the harbor.

It was louder than it should have been.

Ships were coming and going at odd hours.

Lanterns flickered in patterns that didn’t match tide or schedule.

Crews moved fast, heads down, loading without the usual arguments.

Kael counted hulls the way he counted exits.

One.

Two.

Four.

Too many.

He watched a brigantine slip away from the quay without ceremony, sails catching wind like they’d been waiting.

Another ship arrived barely an hour later, its crew thinner than expected, eyes hollow, voices sharp with relief.

He listened harder.

Whispers traveled up from the street in pieces. “—took three nights—” “—tentacles, swear to—” “—kraken or something like it—” “—paid double if you don’t ask—” Kael’s jaw tightened.

Sea monsters weren’t new.

Sailors always had stories.

But these weren’t boasts or excuses.

They were recruitment pitches.

Desperation wrapped in bravado.

Ships were losing people.

Ships were hiring anyone.

He filed that away.

A knock came at his door—light, practiced.

Kael didn’t move.

Another knock.

Then a voice, soft and amused. “Relax,” a woman said. “If I wanted you dead, I wouldn’t knock.” Kael crossed the room without sound and opened the door just enough to see her.

She smiled like someone who knew exactly what kind of trouble he was and didn’t mind. “You’re the quiet one,” she said. “Paid for the room, not the company.” “I needed a place to think,” Kael replied. “Everyone does,” she said. “Some people think louder.” She leaned against the frame, eyes flicking past him into the room.

She noticed the shifted wardrobe.

The loosened bar.

She approved. “Harbor’s busy,” she said casually. “That’s usually a bad sign.” Kael met her gaze. “People talk.” “They always do,” she agreed. “Lately, they don’t come back.” She straightened. “You’ll want to keep the window closed after midnight.

Watch doesn’t like silhouettes.” “Thanks,” Kael said.

She smiled once more and walked away, heels light on the stairs.

Kael closed the door and sat back down.

From the bed, he could hear the city trying to forget him.

From the window, he could hear the sea offering something worse.

He stayed there for three nights.

Each night, more ships.

More whispers.

More coin changing hands without receipts.

Crews that didn’t ask questions and captains who didn’t answer them.

On the fourth night, he saw a ship that made the knowing tighten.

Not because it was powerful.

Because it was prepared.

Kael leaned back from the window and exhaled slowly.

The city wasn’t done with him.

But the sea didn’t care who he was.

And that made it dangerous in a way Gravewater never could.
