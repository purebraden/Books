# Chapter 3 — Marks That Push Back

Gravewater didn’t mourn the way inland towns did.

It adjusted.

Mirek’s absence showed up in small ways first.

The way crowds closed differently.

The way arguments didn’t draw eyes as easily.

Mirek had been loud by design.

Without him, the noise flattened.

That made everything else sharper.

Kael felt it on the docks the morning after they buried what was left of him.

There was no grave.

Just a weighted sack and the long drop beyond the breakwater, done quietly before the tide turned.

Bram hadn’t spoken through the whole thing.

Tessa had watched the water like she expected it to answer.

Now the quay moved around Kael like a body missing a limb.

Familiar, but wrong.

He didn’t work the crowd.

He watched.

Two guards lingered near the weigh house again.

Same ones as before.

They weren’t stopping anyone.

They weren’t pretending to work.

They were waiting for something to happen.

Kael didn’t give it to them.

By midday, Bram found him near the cooper’s shop, rubbing his hands together the way he did when he was nervous or thinking too fast. “We need coin,” Bram said. “We always need coin,” Kael replied. “Not like this,” Bram said. “Lowwake’s drying up.

People are scared.

Marks are light.

And there’s talk.” Kael looked at him. “About us?” he asked.

Bram hesitated just long enough to answer the question he hadn’t asked. “About the paper.” Kael felt the familiar tightening in his chest. “What kind of talk?” “The kind that doesn’t use names,” Bram said. “The kind that ends conversations.” Tessa joined them, eyes already narrowed. “I told you waiting was a bad idea.” Kael shook his head. “Waiting was the only idea that didn’t make us visible.” “And now?” she asked.

Kael didn’t answer right away.

They moved to the loft and closed the shutters.

The room felt emptier without Mirek’s noise.

Bram sat on his pallet.

Tessa leaned against the wall.

Kael stood. “There’s a runner,” Bram said. “From the Charter Hold.

Comes down the salt road twice a week.

Carries sealed packets.

He’s protected.” “Protected how?” Tessa asked. “Watch rotation,” Bram said. “Not close, but timed.

He moves when patrols cross.” Kael exhaled slowly. “No.” Bram looked up sharply. “We need coin.” “We need to stay alive,” Kael said. “Protected marks aren’t for crews missing a man.” “Protected marks are for crews that know the timing,” Bram shot back. “We know the timing.” Kael looked at Tessa.

She didn’t meet his eyes.

That told him everything. “How did you hear about this?” Kael asked.

Bram’s jaw tightened. “I listen.” “Who told you,” Kael said.

Bram swallowed. “Someone who wanted it known.” That settled it.

Kael turned away and paced the narrow room.

Protected marks weren’t just harder.

They were louder.

When they went wrong, they didn’t go wrong quietly.

But Bram was right about one thing.

Coin was drying up.

Fear did that.

And fear didn’t pay rent. “We don’t touch the packets,” Kael said finally. “We take the runner’s purse only.

Clean.

In and out.” “And if the Watch moves?” Tessa asked. “Then we don’t,” Kael said. “No heroics.” Bram nodded too quickly.

Tessa didn’t nod at all.

They scouted the route that afternoon.

The runner came off the salt road at the same time each day, coat buttoned high, satchel slung tight.

He walked with purpose and didn’t look around.

Guards passed him without acknowledgment.

Kael counted steps.

Corners.

Blind spots.

The rhythm wasn’t perfect, but it was workable.

That was what scared him.

They moved at dusk, when the light went flat and shadows lied.

Tessa took position near a stack of barrels, eyes on the patrols.

Bram drifted wide, ready to cut off any pursuit.

Kael slipped into the crowd and let the noise swallow him.

The runner came into view, head down, one hand on the satchel strap.

Kael adjusted his pace and felt the old calm settle in.

This part he knew.

The patrol passed.

Kael moved.

He brushed the runner’s shoulder and felt resistance immediately.

Not cloth.

A hand.

The runner turned.

Too fast.

Kael twisted, fingers snapping back empty as the runner’s elbow drove into his ribs.

Air left Kael’s lungs in a sharp burst.

The runner shouted. “Thief!” Everything went loud.

Guards moved.

Not casually now.

Purposeful.

One reached for Kael’s arm.

Kael ducked and rolled, came up running.

Bram stepped in too early.

He grabbed the runner’s satchel and yanked, hard.

The strap snapped.

The satchel fell.

The runner screamed again, louder.

Tessa swore.

Kael saw the mistake even as it happened.

They hadn’t planned for resistance.

They hadn’t planned for the runner to fight back.

They hadn’t planned for eyes.

Kael shoved through the crowd and broke left.

Bram hesitated, then bolted after him, satchel in hand.

Tessa followed last, glancing back once too often.

They scattered into Lowwake and didn’t stop running until their lungs burned.

They regrouped an hour later in a derelict storage shed near the old foundations.

Bram dropped the satchel between them, breathing hard. “Worth it?” Tessa asked.

Bram grinned, wild-eyed. “Worth it.” Kael opened the satchel.

Inside were sealed packets.

Heavy paper.

Official marks.

And a small pouch of coin, heavier than he’d expected.

Kael closed the satchel. “We don’t open these,” he said.

Bram’s grin faded. “We already crossed the line.” “We crossed it,” Kael said. “We don’t sprint across it.” They argued in low voices until the sound of boots outside cut them short.

Three guards passed the shed.

One paused.

Sniffed the air.

They didn’t look inside.

Kael didn’t breathe until they were gone.

They waited until full dark to move again.

When they returned to the loft, Kael barred the door and sat with his back against it. “We give this back,” Tessa said quietly. “Quietly.

Through someone who can.” Kael looked at Bram.

Bram didn’t meet his eyes.

That night, Kael slept poorly.

Dreams of water and paper and hands reaching where they shouldn’t.

By morning, Bram was gone.

So was the satchel.

Kael found the door unbarred.

He didn’t swear.

He didn’t shout.

He sat on the pallet and stared at the empty space where the satchel had been.

Tessa stood in the doorway, pale. “He sold it,” she said. “Or tried to,” Kael replied.

They found Bram near midday.

Not drowned.

Not beaten.

Executed.

His throat had been cut clean, body laid out where someone wanted it found.

The satchel was gone.

The Watch stood nearby, faces unreadable. “Inter-gang violence,” one guard said when asked. “Happens.” Kael looked at Bram’s open eyes and said nothing.

That night, the slums went quiet in a way Kael had never heard before.

And somewhere in that silence, something began to move that did not care about guards, or paper, or coin.
