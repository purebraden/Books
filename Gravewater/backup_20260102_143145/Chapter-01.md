# Chapter 1 — Low Hands, Fast Eyes

Kael learned the docks the way some people learned scripture: by repetition, correction, and consequence.

Gravewater’s lower quay woke before the sun.

By the time the light crept over the Stone Rise, the docks were already loud—men shouting weights, women cursing prices, gulls screaming over scraps.

Nets came in heavy with the night’s work, slick and dark with fish that still twitched.

The smell of salt and rot clung to everything.

It was honest in that way.

Nothing here pretended to be clean.

Kael moved through it with his head down and his shoulders slightly wrong.

He’d practiced the posture until it was instinct.

Too straight and you were confident.

Too loose and you were careless.

The trick was to look tired without looking weak.

Tired people were invisible.

He passed a pair of dockhands arguing over a torn net, stepped around a cart leaking brine, and slipped between two sailors hauling a crate.

His eyes never stopped moving.

He watched reflections in puddles, the sway of belts, the way hands rested near knives.

Most men wore their danger openly.

Guards were easier.

Their threat announced itself in uniform and posture.

The real problem was the ones who didn’t need to prove anything.

Mirek was already ahead of him, loud and obvious near the fishmongers.

Mirek had a gift for attention.

He argued badly on purpose, miscounted coins, complained about weights that were plainly marked.

He drew eyes like a fire drew moths, then burned just enough to be dismissed. “Shorted,” Mirek was saying now, voice pitched high with irritation. “You call that a stone?

I’ve hauled stones heavier than that with one arm.” The fishmonger rolled his eyes. “Take it or don’t.” “Don’t get clever,” Mirek said. “I’m not paying—” Kael didn’t listen past that.

Mirek would keep going until the fishmonger snapped, then laugh it off.

That was the cue.

People watched arguments.

They didn’t watch what slid past them while they did.

Tessa leaned against a piling near the rope stacks, arms folded, hair pulled back tight.

Anyone else would have looked bored.

Kael knew better.

Her eyes flicked constantly—not at people directly, but at surfaces.

Water.

Polished wood.

Glass in a tavern window.

She watched what things reflected, not what they showed.

Bram wasn’t visible at all, which meant he was exactly where he should be.

Kael slowed and counted breaths.

One.

Two.

Three.

The mark came off the salt road at an angle that suggested purpose.

His boots were too clean for dock work, but the leather was worn thin at the toe.

Not rich.

Not poor.

A man who walked where he shouldn’t often enough to scuff his boots, but not often enough to replace them.

His coat was decent, dark wool cut to last, but the cuffs were frayed where his hands brushed them.

Paper-handler.

Clerk or factor’s runner.

Someone who carried things that mattered to people who mattered more.

The man touched his inside pocket every few steps.

Not consciously.

Habit.

Kael adjusted his course, letting the crowd close around him.

Two sailors passed with baskets between them, arguing about whose catch was heavier.

A woman laughed too loudly at nothing.

Mirek raised his voice another notch. “—robbery is what it is—” Kael slid in.

The inside pocket was buttoned, not tied.

Sloppy.

Kael’s fingers found the button, turned it, and lifted just enough to slide the folded leather wallet free.

No rush.

Rushing was how you snagged cloth or brushed skin.

The clerk shifted his weight.

Kael shifted with him, keeping contact indirect.

Wallet gone.

Kael kept moving.

He didn’t look back.

He never did.

By the time he reached the rope stacks, the pressure behind him eased.

That moment—when no one was following because no one yet knew they should—was always the same.

Like stepping out of cold water.

Relief, then caution.

Relief was dangerous if you held it too long.

Tessa pushed off the piling and fell into step beside him.

She didn’t speak.

Her head tilted just enough to ask a question. “Clear,” Kael murmured.

She nodded once and peeled away, drifting back toward the quay.

She would watch for a while longer, just in case.

Kael cut left into a side lane between warehouses where the air grew cooler and the noise dulled.

Mirek caught up, grinning, already rubbing his hands together. “Clean,” Mirek said. “Didn’t even flinch.” “Not yet,” Kael said.

They didn’t stop until they reached the fish sheds.

The walls there were thick, stone patched with wood, the doors warped from damp.

Inside, it smelled of old blood and smoke.

Kael ducked through an open door and finally unfolded the wallet.

Coin first.

Not much.

Enough to matter to the clerk, not enough to draw notice on its own.

Kael divided it automatically, three equal stacks, muscle memory doing the work while his eyes moved on.

Paper.

The wallet held more than one sheet, folded tight.

Kael slid them free and scanned them once, then again more slowly.

His breath caught, just a little.

It wasn’t a receipt.

Not a cargo list.

The paper was thin and stiff, creased wrong, like it had been folded and unfolded too many times.

Lines of writing ran down the page in a tight, efficient hand.

Times.

Names.

Tide marks.

Short notes in the margins.

Kael recognized two of the names immediately.

Dock foremen.

One of them had drowned last winter when a line snapped during a storm.

The official story was bad luck.

The unofficial one was worse. “Tess,” Kael said quietly, keeping his voice low. “Who was watching the salt road?” Tessa frowned. “No one I didn’t know.

Two Watch up by the weigh house, but not close.

Why?” Kael folded the paper and slid it into his sleeve instead of the pouch at his belt. “Problem?” Mirek asked. “Maybe,” Kael said. “Maybe not.” Mirek shrugged and scooped his share of the coin. “Coin’s coin.” Kael handed him the rest without comment.

Fair was fair.

For now.

They split without ceremony.

That was another rule.

Don’t leave together.

Don’t leave in a line.

Don’t leave looking like you were friends.

Kael took the long way back toward Lowwake, winding through alleys that smelled of rot and wet wood.

He passed a shrine with no offerings and a doorway where someone slept wrapped in sailcloth.

He didn’t pull the paper out again until he was alone.

The schedule ended with a mark he didn’t recognize, but the tide notation beside it made his stomach tighten.

Deep Reach.

Night tide.

Kael folded the paper and pressed it flat against his ribs.

He didn’t know what it meant yet.

He only knew it wasn’t meant to be stolen.

That was worse.

The sleeping loft he shared with Bram sat above a cooper’s shop, reached by a narrow stair that creaked if you stepped wrong.

Kael paused at the bottom and listened.

No voices.

No footsteps behind him.

He climbed anyway, slow and quiet.

Bram was inside, sitting on the edge of his pallet, counting something under his breath.

He looked up when Kael entered. “Good?” Bram asked. “Clean,” Kael said.

Bram nodded and didn’t ask more.

That was Bram’s strength.

He knew when questions cost more than answers.

They ate dried fish and stale bread in silence.

Kael’s mind kept circling the paper, the names, the tide marks.

He didn’t mention it.

Not yet.

Information was leverage only if you knew how to use it.

Otherwise it was just weight.

Later, lying on his back, Kael stared at the low ceiling and listened to the harbor sounds drift through the shutters.

Creaking wood.

Water against stone.

Somewhere, far out, a bell rang once and stopped.

Tomorrow, he wouldn’t take ledger men.

Tomorrow, he’d take something safer.

The docks didn’t care what you planned, but they noticed what you learned.

And Kael had learned enough to be careful.
