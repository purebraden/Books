PROLOGUE

Kael learned hunger before he learned numbers.

It arrived first as a sound---a thin ringing behind the eyes that made
the world feel too loud and too close. Voices scraped. Light pressed.
Even stillness felt heavy, like something waiting to fall. Later, people
would tell him hunger lived in the belly. They were wrong. Hunger lived
higher than that. It lived where decisions were made.

The city taught him this without words.

Gravewater was always damp, even when it hadn't rained. Stone held the
memory of water the way wood held rot. Lines had been carved into the
lower walls of buildings---some deliberate, some accidental---marking
where the water had reached in years past. No one bothered to erase
them. They weren't warnings. They were records.

Kael was eight when he noticed that the marks never went down.

At that age, he slept where he could: under stairs when they were dry,
in net sheds when the fishermen forgot to lock them, in a narrow space
between two leaning buildings

where the wind didn't quite reach. He learned quickly which places
flooded first and which smells meant you should leave even if it meant
walking all night.

He also learned which adults looked through you and which ones looked at
you.

The woman with the bread stall was the latter.

She worked the morning corner near the lift platform, close enough to
the docks that sailors passed by but far enough that they weren't her
problem anymore. Her hands shook when she stacked loaves. Not
badly---just enough to notice if you paid attention. Kael paid attention
to hands. Hands told you what a person could still afford to lose.

He watched her for three days before he acted.

On the fourth morning, the ringing behind his eyes grew sharp enough
that it felt like pressure. He stood there longer than he meant to,
staring at the steam curling from the fresh bread, counting breaths the
way he did when he needed to slow himself down.

She turned to argue with a supplier.

Kael reached out and took a loaf.

It was warm through the thin cloth of his shirt, heavier than he
expected. He didn't run right away. He walked---fast, but not
panicked---until the corner was out of sight. Only then did he run, feet
slipping on wet stone, heart hammering in his ears.

He didn't stop until the city opened up beneath him, the lower channels
spreading out like dark veins. He crouched behind a broken railing and
waited, listening for shouts.

None came.

His hands shook as he tore the loaf in half. He ate slowly, carefully,
the way he'd learned to eat anything that might be

the last thing you found that day. The warmth eased the ringing, just a
little.

That was when the older boy spoke.

"You going to finish that?"

Kael nearly dropped the bread.

The boy sat on the railing a few steps away, legs dangling over the
drop, posture loose in a way that meant he'd already decided where the
edges were. He didn't look angry. He didn't look impressed. He looked
curious, which was worse.

Kael shook his head and held out the smaller piece.

The boy took it, ate it in three bites, and wiped his hands on his
trousers. "You took from the tired one."

Kael said nothing.

The boy nodded, as if this confirmed something he'd suspected. "They
notice," he said. "Even when they don't chase you."

"What should I take?" Kael asked.

The boy smiled, thin and sharp. "From people who can afford to be
angry."

Then he jumped down and walked away, leaving Kael alone with the last of
the bread and a thought that would not settle.

Kael watched the water for a long time after that, listening to it slide
past the pylons. The city creaked above him, wood shifting against
stone, always adjusting. He understood, dimly, that the bread hadn't
been free. Someone had paid for it. Just not him.

That was the lesson Gravewater repeated in different forms as he grew.

He learned that some doors opened quietly and some announced themselves
no matter how careful you were. He learned that repairs were never meant
to last, only to hold long enough for something else to break first. He
learned

that people spoke about the future only in short distances---until the
next tide, until the next platform adjustment, until the next
inspection.

He learned to watch.

By ten, he could tell which planks would creak before he stepped on
them. By twelve, he knew which Watch patrols followed patterns and which
enjoyed deviation. By fourteen, he understood that most arguments
weren't about truth but about where the cost would land when things went
wrong.

He noticed when a deal felt heavy even before anyone spoke. He noticed
when a smile meant relief and when it meant relief had already been
spent.

He did not have a name for this habit. He only knew that listening kept
him fed.

On nights when the wind came in off the water just right, carrying the
smell of salt and old metal, Kael would wake with the ringing behind his
eyes and sit up, waiting for it to pass. He would think of the bread
stall, of the lines on the walls, of the way the water never quite
stayed where it belonged.

Gravewater did not teach kindness.

It taught accounting.

And Kael learned early that if you did not choose where the weight fell,
the city would choose for you.

CHAPTER 1

The job was small enough to be honest about.

That alone made Kael uneasy.

They met before dawn on a narrow platform that hadn't bothered
pretending it was level in years. The water below moved in slow, uneven
bands, slapping gently against the

pylons with the sound of something patient testing a boundary. Tessa
stood near the edge, boots planted wide, watching reflections rather
than the street. Bram leaned against a support beam, hood up, hands
still. Mirek arrived last, loud as always, carrying a sack that clinked
once before he caught it and stopped.

"Relax," Mirek said, grinning. "That was an accident."

Tessa didn't look at him. "Accidents make noise."

"So do you," Mirek replied cheerfully.

Kael watched them with half his attention and the route with the rest.
The lift platform two streets over had cycled twice already---too often
for this hour---which meant someone important was moving, or someone
wanted to look important. Either way, it shortened their window.

"Five minutes," Kael said. "We miss one patrol and catch the next."

Mirek's grin softened into something more professional. "Then we don't
miss."

The target was a storage annex built into the side of a counting house
that no longer counted much of anything. Its original owners had fled
upward years ago, leaving behind records no one trusted and goods no one
wanted to claim. The Watch still made appearances, but out of habit more
than interest.

Bram went first, moving ahead without being told. He checked the latch
with two fingers, paused, then nodded once and stepped aside. Kael
followed, testing the board beneath his foot before committing weight.
It held. Barely.

Inside, the air smelled like old rope and damp paper. The annex had been
divided and redivided over the years, walls added where they were needed
and left when they weren't.

Tessa slipped past Kael and took point, her knife already out but low,
more a reminder than a threat.

Mirek waited until they were all inside before closing the door behind
them. He did it gently. Kael noticed. Mirek noticed that Kael noticed
and winked.

They moved quickly but without hurry. Tessa counted steps under her
breath, stopping just before the third partition. She tapped twice on
the wall, listening. Nothing hollow. She shifted left, tried again, then
nodded Kael forward.

"Your turn," she murmured.

Kael knelt, feeling the stone with his palm. There---slight warmth where
it shouldn't be. A recent repair. He slid his fingers along the seam
until he found the catch hidden behind a smear of pitch. It gave with a
soft click.

Mirek exhaled quietly. "Every time."

"Every time," Tessa agreed.

They took only what had been agreed upon: sealed tins of lamp oil, two
coils of wire, and a wrapped packet of tools that hadn't seen daylight
in years. No ledgers. No loose goods. No souvenirs.

Kael kept count automatically, not just of items but of time. The
pressure behind his eyes stayed low, a dull awareness rather than a
warning. That was good. Jobs that felt wrong announced themselves early.

On the way out, Mirek caught his sleeve.

"You hear that?" Mirek whispered.

Kael listened. Footsteps. Two sets. Too light for Watch boots, too
regular for dockhands.

Tessa didn't wait. She shifted their exit without comment, guiding them
through a narrow service corridor that sloped downward and then up
again, skirting a channel that smelled faintly of rust and salt. Bram
took the rear, pausing once to

adjust a loose plank so it wouldn't complain later.

They emerged two levels over and one down, the city rearranging itself
around them as if nothing had happened.

Only when they were clear did Mirek speak. "See? Easy."

Tessa finally looked at him. "You nearly tripped the wire."

"Nearly," Mirek said. "Didn't."

Kael handed Mirek one of the tins. "You rushed."

Mirek blinked, then shrugged. "Felt like we had time."

Kael met his eyes. "We didn't."

Mirek considered that, then nodded. "All right."

That was as close as Mirek ever came to apology.

They regrouped in a dry alcove near the old fish stairs, where someone
had once painted a sign promising fresh catch daily. The paint had
peeled away long ago, leaving only the word fresh ghosted on the stone.

Tessa broke open a small packet and passed it around. Flatbread,
slightly stale but serviceable. Kael took a piece and ate slowly,
listening to the city resume its normal sounds.

Mirek launched into a story about a job years ago that had gone nowhere
and meant nothing. Bram listened without comment, eyes tracking movement
along the water. Tessa checked the wire coil twice, then once more for
good measure.

Kael watched them all and felt the quiet satisfaction of something done
properly. No excess. No drama. Just work.

For a moment---only a moment---the city felt manageable.

They split up after, as they always did, melting back into Gravewater's
shifting routes. Kael headed upward, following a path that would take
him past the lift and then around it, avoiding attention without
pretending to.

As he walked, he noticed a man arguing with a Watch patrol

over a crate that had shifted during the night. The Watchman's voice was
calm, practiced. The man's wasn't. Kael didn't stop, but he listened
long enough to know how it would end.

The pressure behind his eyes stirred faintly, then settled.

He reached the stairs and climbed, the sound of water fading as stone
took over. Above him, Gravewater adjusted again, always finding new ways
to hold.

Kael moved with it, as he always had, unaware of how fragile that
balance truly was.

CHAPTER 2

Mirek didn't miss meetings.

He was late often enough to make it a joke, but he never failed to show.
Even on bad days. Especially on bad days. If he said he would be
somewhere, he was there---complaining, grinning, carrying something he
shouldn't have, but present.

So when the sun climbed and Mirek didn't appear, Kael noticed.

He didn't say anything at first. He followed the route they'd agreed on,
took the long way around the lift, paused at the dry stairwell that
smelled faintly of old oil and salt. He waited the length of three
breaths. Then five. Then ten.

The pressure behind his eyes stirred---not sharp, not painful. Just
present.

Bram arrived next, silent as ever. He took one look at Kael and didn't
ask the obvious question.

"Tessa?" Bram murmured.

"On her way," Kael said.

They waited.

The city went on around them. A fisherman argued with a

supplier over a cracked crate. Somewhere above, a platform groaned under
a shift of weight. Water slid against stone, patient and unbothered.

Tessa came from the south route, moving faster than she needed to. She
stopped short when she saw their faces.

"He didn't double back," she said immediately. "I checked."

Kael nodded. "He didn't come through here either."

Silence settled between them, heavier than it should have been.

Bram spoke first. "Where was he last?"

Tessa answered without hesitation. "Lower channel. Old service cut near
the counting houses."

Kael felt the faint tightening in his chest before his mind caught up.
Mirek had mentioned that route the night before, offhand, like it meant
nothing.

"We check," Kael said.

They didn't argue.

The lower levels smelled different in the morning. Cooler. Staler. The
tide had gone out just enough to leave dark stains along the base of the
pylons, slick and uneven. Their boots left faint prints that filled with
water almost immediately.

As they descended, Kael noticed small things: a crate tipped on its side
that hadn't been there yesterday, a length of rope trailing into the
water, cut clean rather than frayed. He told himself not to assign
meaning yet.

The service cut lay where Tessa said it would, half-hidden behind a
collapsed barrier someone had never bothered to fix properly. Bram
slipped through first, then Kael, then Tessa.

The smell hit them all at once.

Tessa froze.

Kael knew before he saw.

Mirek lay half in the water, one arm twisted beneath him, the other
stretched out as if he'd tried to grab something that wasn't there. His
pack was gone. His boots were still on.

For a moment, none of them moved.

Then Tessa swore softly and knelt, fingers hovering just above Mirek's
shoulder before settling there, careful and precise even now.

"He's cold," she said.

Bram turned away, scanning the channel, eyes sharp and searching. Kael
crouched at the edge of the water, staring at the way Mirek's reflection
broke apart with every small movement of the tide.

Kael's hands shook as he reached into his own pocket without realizing
it, checking for something that wasn't there.

He catalogued details instead.

The way Mirek's head was angled wrong. The scrape marks on the stone
nearby, shallow but fresh. The water moving faster than it should have
in such a narrow cut.

Tessa stood abruptly. "We can't stay."

"Not yet," Kael said.

She looked at him, something hot and sharp flickering in her eyes. For a
heartbeat, Kael thought she might say something she couldn't take back.

Instead, she inhaled slowly and nodded. "Five minutes."

They worked without speaking.

Bram checked Mirek's pockets, then shook his head once. Tessa closed
Mirek's eyes. Kael adjusted his position just enough that the current
wouldn't pull him further under.

Kael felt a heavy, stupid guilt settle in his chest. He replayed the
previous day without meaning to---the moment Mirek had rushed, the way
Kael had corrected him, the casual confidence Mirek had shrugged into
place afterward.

If Kael had said more.

If he had insisted.

If he had gone with him.

None of it mattered. The city did not care about if.

A dockworker appeared at the far end of the channel, paused, then
quickly turned away.

Bram noticed. "Witness."

"Won't talk," Tessa said flatly.

"No," Kael agreed. "But he saw something."

Tessa straightened, jaw set. "We move him."

They lifted Mirek carefully, the weight of him wrong in Kael's arms. He
had always been heavier when alive, all motion and presence. Now he felt
like something the city had already started to reclaim.

They carried him to higher ground, to a place where water didn't reach
as easily. They laid him out, straightening his coat, arranging him as
best they could.

Kael stood back, suddenly unsure what to do with his hands.

The city did not pause. People passed at a distance, eyes sliding away.
Somewhere, someone laughed.

Tessa wiped her hands on her trousers and turned sharply. "This wasn't
an accident."

Bram met her gaze. "We don't know that yet."

Tessa laughed once, harsh. "You don't die like that by slipping."

Kael closed his eyes briefly. The pressure behind them swelled, then
settled again, like something testing a boundary he wasn't ready to
cross.

"We leave," Kael said quietly. "Now."

Tessa looked at him, searching his face. "And then?"

Kael had no answer that mattered.

They moved away from the channel, leaving Mirek behind with a marker
only Gravewater would recognize. As they climbed, Kael felt the absence
immediately---the timing off, the spacing wrong, the silence too wide
where Mirek's voice should have been.

The crew had lost a piece.

And the city did not care.

CHAPTER 3

Gravewater did not care that Mirek was dead.

It didn't slow. It didn't hush. It didn't even shift its rhythms in a
way anyone would call respect. The water kept moving as it always had,
pressing into cracks and pulling away again, leaving damp lines on stone
like punctuation no one read anymore. Platforms creaked under the same
familiar weights. Dockhands shouted the same insults. The Watch made
their same measured rounds, boots striking wet boards with the
confidence of men who believed the city still belonged to them.

Only the crew's timing was wrong.

They met that morning because habit demanded it. Not the full crew. Just
the three of them, standing in a narrow cut between a net shed and a
storage wall where the wind couldn't quite reach. It was one of Mirek's
preferred spots---dry enough, hidden enough, close enough to the lower
channels that you could hear trouble before it found you.

Tessa arrived first and didn't sit.

Kael arrived second and did, because his legs insisted on routine even
when his mind refused it. Bram was already there, leaning in a shadow
that looked like it had been made

for him.

No one spoke for a long time.

The silence didn't feel peaceful. It felt unfinished.

Kael noticed how they stood without Mirek. Tessa's posture filled space
aggressively, as if daring the city to dispute her claim. Bram took less
space than usual, shoulders angled, attention outward rather than
inward. Kael sat and watched them both, his hands resting on his knees
where they couldn't betray him by shaking.

He kept thinking of Mirek's weight in his arms. How wrong it had felt.
How light.

Tessa broke the silence first. "We're short."

Bram nodded once. Kael didn't argue, because there was nothing to argue
with.

"There's a run I had lined up," Tessa continued. "Not worth it now."

Kael glanced up. "Which one?"

"West platform, oil tins and wire. Same kind of thing Mirek liked," she
said, and her voice tightened on the last words. "Requires someone who
can carry and someone who can talk."

Kael felt the smallest sting behind his eyes, sharp and brief. He
breathed through it.

"We don't take it," Kael said.

Tessa's jaw flexed. "We can't afford to start saying no."

"We can't afford to start getting caught," Bram replied.

Tessa shot him a look. Bram didn't flinch.

Kael watched them and realized something he didn't like: Mirek had been
the buffer. The loudness. The part that turned sharp edges into jokes.
Without him, every disagreement landed clean.

"We take smaller work," Kael said. "One-person jobs. Low

risk."

Tessa's laugh was short. "Low risk doesn't feed you."

Kael didn't answer immediately. He was looking past her toward the open
street where a Watch patrol passed in the distance, lanterns swinging.
The pattern was wrong.

They weren't supposed to be here this early.

Kael felt the pressure behind his eyes stir, not pain yet, more like a
fingertip tracing the inside of his skull.

"You notice that?" he asked.

Bram followed his gaze. "Patrol's off."

Tessa glanced once and dismissed it with a shrug that sounded louder
than it should have. "Maybe they changed it."

"They don't change it," Bram said.

Tessa's eyes narrowed. "They do if they want someone to notice."

Kael felt his stomach tighten. That was the kind of sentence you didn't
say unless you already believed the worst.

"We move," Kael said.

They split without ceremony. Kael took the outer route first, not
because it was the safest but because it showed him the most. Gravewater
was built in layers, each level a compromise between what was needed and
what could be held. Most people moved in straight lines when they could.
Kael moved in curves, letting the city reveal itself in fragments: a
broken railing repaired with rope instead of wood, a fresh chalk mark on
a doorframe, a merchant counting coins twice with a hand that shook too
much.

He watched hands. He watched eyes. He watched where people chose to
stand.

He saw someone watching him.

Not openly. Not like a Watchman. Just a man leaning at the

corner of a stairwell, pretending to be interested in a basket of dried
fish. His gaze tracked Kael a moment too long, then dropped away as if
embarrassed.

Kael didn't change pace. He didn't acknowledge the man. He just let the
detail settle.

By the time Kael reached the meeting place near the lift, the pressure
behind his eyes had become a dull ache. He tried to blame it on poor
sleep, on grief, on the fact that his jaw had been clenched for two days
straight.

Tessa was already there, standing near the edge of a platform where the
boards bowed slightly under the weight of too many feet. Bram arrived
shortly after, quiet and sharp.

"You're being followed," Bram said without preamble.

Kael nodded. "I saw one. You?"

"Tessa?" Bram asked.

Tessa's expression didn't change. "Two. Different faces."

Kael exhaled slowly. "So it's not just Watch."

Bram's gaze hardened. "No."

Tessa shifted her stance. "We need to decide what to do with the
ledger."

Kael felt his stomach dip. He'd almost forgotten it existed in the space
between Mirek's death and the city's persistence. Almost.

"It's still hidden," Kael said.

"For now," Tessa replied. "But Mirek's dead. People talk when someone
dies. People ask why."

Bram spoke carefully. "We dump it."

Tessa's eyes flashed. "We don't even know what it's worth."

"We know what it costs," Bram said.

Kael didn't speak right away. He thought of the object under the boards.
Not as paper, not as ink, but as weight. A knot tied into something
larger. He hated that his mind framed it

like that.

He stood. "We move it."

Bram's head tilted. "Where?"

"Not here," Kael said.

Tessa nodded once. "Agreed."

They didn't argue about where to take it in the open. Gravewater had
ears, and the people who listened were rarely the ones you saw. Kael led
them through two levels and across a narrow bridge that rocked gently
over a channel. Tessa kept her hand near her knife. Bram walked with his
hood down for once, letting his face be seen by anyone watching---an old
trick, and not a comforting one. It was a signal: we know you're there.

They reached a small room they used sometimes, a place above a shuttered
chandlery where the air smelled of wax and damp wood. Mirek had
complained about the smell every time they came here. The memory hit
Kael like a sudden cold draft, sharp enough that he had to steady
himself against the wall.

Tessa noticed. Her eyes softened for a fraction of a second, then
hardened again as if she regretted the softness.

Kael pulled up the floorboard and retrieved the wrapped ledger.

It felt heavier than paper should.

Or maybe his hands had changed.

Bram watched him closely. "You all right?"

Kael nodded. "Just tired."

It wasn't a lie, but it wasn't the whole truth either.

They argued in low voices. Not about whether the ledger was
dangerous---that was agreed. They argued about what dangerous meant in
Gravewater.

Tessa wanted to sell it fast, to turn risk into coin before the city
could turn it into blood. Bram wanted it gone entirely, burned, drowned,
erased from their lives.

Kael wanted time.

He didn't say that.

Instead he said, "We don't know who wants it."

Tessa's mouth tightened. "Someone killed Mirek."

Bram's voice stayed flat. "Someone killed Mirek and took his pack."

Kael looked down at the ledger. His fingers pressed lightly on the wrap.

"Then they'll come looking," Kael said.

Silence.

Outside, the city creaked.

Tessa spoke first, careful now. "We should leave."

Kael met her gaze. "Leave where?"

Tessa's eyes flicked away. "Up. Out. Anywhere."

Bram didn't laugh this time. He simply said, "There is no out in
Gravewater. Only other layers."

Kael felt the dull ache behind his eyes tighten again, as if the city
itself agreed.

"We can't leave yet," Kael said.

Tessa stared at him. "Because of the ledger?"

Kael hesitated, and in that hesitation he realized he hadn't answered
honestly.

Because of Mirek. Because of the sense that leaving now would turn his
death into something tidy. Because Kael didn't want tidy. He wanted
accounting.

"We can't leave yet," he repeated, and hated how weak it sounded.

Bram stepped closer. "Then we decide quickly. Because

they're closing in."

Kael nodded. He wrapped the ledger again, tighter this time, and slid it
into a new hiding place---higher, drier, harder to reach. When it was
done, he sat back and listened.

There were footsteps on the stairs outside.

Not loud. Not hurried.

Just present.

Tessa's hand drifted to her knife. Bram moved to the window and looked
down into the street.

Kael didn't move. He felt his pulse in his throat, steady but heavy. The
pressure behind his eyes sharpened, and for a heartbeat he thought the
knowing might break through---might tell him who was on the stairs, why
they were there, what they wanted.

He refused it.

The footsteps paused.

Then they continued past, moving upward, as if the person had changed
their mind.

Or as if they wanted the crew to know they could have stopped.

Bram turned from the window. "They're playing with us."

Tessa's smile was thin and humorless. "Welcome to being important."

Kael looked at the floor where the ledger was hidden and felt the first
real understanding settle in his bones:

Mirek's death had not been an end.

It had been an opening.

And the city had begun to look through it.

CHAPTER 4

The first door didn't open.

It should have. Kael had used it a dozen times over the years---a narrow
service entry tucked between a cooper's shop and a shuttered alehouse,
its latch worn smooth by hands that knew where to press. It had never
been locked, not truly. It existed in that category of Gravewater
architecture that survived because everyone agreed not to draw attention
to it.

Kael pressed the latch.

Nothing.

He tried again, slower this time, fingers feeling for the familiar give.
The wood was damp, swollen just enough to change the tolerances. Or it
had been reinforced. Either way, it did not yield.

Behind him, Tessa exhaled sharply. "That's new."

Bram stepped closer, eyes scanning the street. "We're exposed."

Kael nodded and stepped back, letting the door remain closed. Doors that
refused to open usually had reasons, and most of those reasons involved
people watching to see what you'd do next.

They moved on without comment, turning down a side route that ran closer
to the water than Kael preferred. The channel below was higher than it
should have been at this hour, lapping at the pylons with an irregular
rhythm that set his teeth on edge. The city smelled sharper here---salt
and wet iron, like something had been disturbed and not settled back
into place.

Tessa took the lead again, her pace quickening. She didn't like detours.
Detours meant variables, and variables were where people got hurt.

"This is bad timing," she muttered.

"It's intentional timing," Bram replied.

Kael didn't add anything. He was busy noticing how sound carried.
Footsteps echoed farther than they should have, bouncing off stone and
water alike, blurring direction. Voices from two levels up drifted down
with unnatural clarity, words flattening as they traveled.

Gravewater was listening.

They reached the corner where a runner usually waited---an old man with
a permanent limp and a habit of asking questions he already knew the
answers to. He wasn't there.

Kael stopped. So did the others.

Tessa frowned. "He doesn't miss."

"No," Bram agreed. "He leaves."

They stood there for a moment longer than comfort allowed, the absence
stretching thin. Kael felt the pressure behind his eyes begin to gather,
not pain yet, just a sense of wrongness tightening like a drawn cord.

"We don't use him," Kael said.

Tessa shot him a look. "That was our fastest route."

"And now it isn't," Kael replied.

She didn't argue, but her jaw set in a way Kael recognized. Speed was
her answer to most problems. It always had been.

They adjusted course again, heading upward toward a level that smelled
faintly of old soap and hot metal. Kael hated moving up when things went
wrong. The higher levels pretended to be cleaner, safer, more
stable---but they were also watched more closely. People noticed who
didn't belong.

A Watch patrol rounded the corner ahead of them, three men walking too
slowly for their route to be casual. Lanterns hung low, casting light
that seemed to cling to surfaces longer than it should.

Tessa swore under her breath.

"Split," Bram said quietly.

Kael shook his head. "Not yet."

They slowed, becoming just another trio moving with the city's flow. The
patrol passed close enough that Kael could see the scuff marks on one
man's boots---fresh, the wrong kind for this level. The Watchman glanced
at Kael's face, held the look a fraction too long, then moved on.

Tessa didn't breathe until they were clear. "They're interrupting, not
intercepting."

"Testing," Bram said. "Seeing how we react."

Kael felt the faintest surge behind his eyes and pushed it down. He
didn't need to know why yet. He just needed to survive the when.

They ducked into a narrow stairwell that sloped downward and then
leveled out, leading to a small platform overlooking a wider channel.
The water here moved sluggishly, thick with debris, reflecting light in
broken patterns that made Kael's head ache.

"This is where we decide," Tessa said, turning on him. "We can't keep
drifting."

Kael met her gaze. "We don't have enough information."

"We never do," she snapped. "That's not new."

Bram stayed silent, but his eyes moved between them, tracking the shift.

Tessa gestured sharply. "We move the ledger. Now. Somewhere deeper.
Somewhere they won't follow."

Kael felt a chill run through him. "Deeper isn't safer."

"It's quieter," she said.

"Quieter is worse," Kael replied. "It means fewer witnesses."

Tessa's mouth twisted. "You're stalling."

Kael didn't deny it. He looked past her at the water, at the way it
