CHAPTER 26

Pressure didn't arrive with knives anymore.

It arrived with calendars.

Kael felt it the moment he left the counting house. Not eyes on his
back---he'd learned how to live with that---but timing tightening around
him. Messages that arrived an hour earlier than expected. Couriers who
didn't wait. Doors that stayed open just long enough to make a point.

He didn't rush.

Rushing was how you told people where to push.

The first reminder came from the clean job. Not a demand. Not even a
question. A note tucked into a ledger Kael was fencing, written in the
same careful hand as before:

Window unchanged. Confirmation expected.

No threat. No consequence listed.

Just the quiet assumption that he would comply.

The second arrived that night, from a different direction

entirely. A dockside fixer Kael had used once, years ago, sent word
through a boy who didn't know what he was carrying.

"Someone wants to talk," the boy said, eyes darting. "Says it's
friendly."

Kael paid him and didn't go.

The third wasn't a message at all.

It was a warning framed as kindness.

He was crossing a narrow bridge when an older woman selling fried dough
looked up and met his eyes. She didn't smile. She didn't frown.

"Careful, boy," she said softly, as if talking about the slick boards.
"People are counting on you not to trip."

Kael paused just long enough to make it look like he was steadying
himself.

"Thank you," he said.

She nodded once and went back to her pan.

By morning, Kael had three clocks ticking.

None of them cared about the others.

That was the problem.

He spent the day moving through the city without committing to anything.
He watched manifests being copied at dock offices. He watched guards
change shifts early in places they usually didn't. He listened to men
argue about numbers that didn't matter and women whisper about ships
that were always late.

The same name surfaced again and again.

The same boat.

Never central. Never highlighted. Just present in margins, in
corrections, in footnotes where no one expected anything important to
live.

Kael started mapping it in his head.

Routes. Storage yards. Who signed what. Who pretended not to notice when
weights didn't match cargo.

By evening, he understood the shape of it.

This wasn't about a single job.

It was about flow.

And everyone involved wanted him to touch it---just enough to leave
fingerprints, not enough to claim ownership.

Kael stopped at a tea stall near the lower channels and ordered
something hot he didn't need. He leaned against the counter, listening
to dockworkers complain about delays and bribes, letting the knowing
settle into a low, steady hum.

Then he made his choice.

It wasn't dramatic.

He didn't confront anyone.

He performed a demonstration.

That night, a sealed crate vanished from a storage yard three blocks
from the water. Not one of the important ones. Not guarded heavily. Just
valuable enough that someone would miss it quickly and ask questions in
the wrong order.

Kael didn't break locks.

He didn't spill blood.

He moved the crate five feet at a time through blind spots and shadows,
slid it onto a cart that wasn't scheduled to move until morning, and
replaced it with another crate of identical size filled with scrap and
old ballast.

By dawn, the paperwork said nothing had changed.

By midday, everyone knew something had.

The dock office blamed the yard. The yard blamed the manifest. The
manifest blamed a clerk who swore the numbers were right when he wrote
them down.

The crate reappeared that afternoon in a different warehouse, unsealed,
its contents cataloged neatly and left

where they could be found.

Inside was a single note, written in Kael's hand:

I can touch this whenever I want.

The reaction was immediate and silent.

The clean job's reminder did not repeat itself.

The unofficial offer evaporated.

The fixer stopped sending boys.

That evening, Kael received one message.

Not written.

Spoken.

A man sat across from him in a public house that smelled like old wood
and spilled beer. He didn't introduce himself. He didn't reach for
Kael's drink.

"You've made your point," the man said.

Kael sipped slowly. "Which one."

"That you're not panicking. And that you understand the system better
than most of the people who built it."

Kael tilted his head. "That's generous."

"It's accurate," the man said. "Which means we need to stop pulling you
in different directions."

Kael waited.

"We'll consolidate," the man continued. "One line of work. One schedule.
Fewer surprises."

"And if I say no," Kael asked.

The man shrugged. "Then you'll keep breaking small things until we bleed
out in accounting errors."

Kael smiled faintly. "You make it sound like a threat."

The man shook his head. "It's a diagnosis."

They sat in silence for a moment, the noise of the room swelling around
them.

Finally, Kael said, "I'm still taking the job."

"I know."

"But not on your timing."

The man considered this. "What timing do you want."

Kael thought of the boat. Of the manifests. Of the way the city bent
itself to keep certain flows uninterrupted.

"Soon," he said. "And not quietly."

The man's brow furrowed. "That would cause---"

"Movement," Kael finished. "And I need movement."

The man studied him carefully now. "You're planning to leave."

Kael didn't answer.

He didn't need to.

The man exhaled slowly. "Then this is where we part ways."

"Probably," Kael agreed.

The man stood. "When this breaks, it won't be clean."

Kael rose as well. "Nothing here ever is."

They separated without shaking hands.

Later, alone on a rooftop with the water breathing below him, Kael let
himself feel the weight of the choice he'd just made.

The clocks were no longer ticking independently.

They were lining up.

He was injured. Still healing. Still mortal.

But the path had narrowed into something solid beneath his feet.

One last run of jobs.

One theft too big to ignore.

One boat that wouldn't wait.

Kael stared out over Gravewater and felt the knowing settle, patient and
sharp.

He wasn't being hunted.

He was being scheduled.

And soon, very soon, he was going to break the schedule badly enough
that the only thing left to do was leave.

By water.

Before the city decided whether to stop him---or follow.

CHAPTER 27

The job came with margins.

Kael noticed that first---the space built into it. Time windows that
didn't scrape each other raw. Routes that overlapped without tangling.
Payment that didn't need to be rushed because the people behind it
expected the work to take as long as it took.

Margins meant confidence.

Margins also meant more than one hand on the rope.

Kael took the offer anyway.

He didn't acknowledge it directly. He never did. He let it sit where it
had been placed, folded twice, ink clean and dry. He read it, memorized
it, then burned it in a dish meant for food and watched the ash curl
like it had opinions.

Then he went to look at the place.

The annex stood where the mid-tiers leaned toward the water, a building
that had grown outward instead of upward, bolted onto older stone with
newer wood, patched and repatched until it looked like a single thought
from a distance. People passed it every day without noticing. That was
its purpose.

It wasn't guarded like something precious. It was guarded like something
necessary.

Kael watched it for three days.

He learned the rhythm first. Not patrols---those were easy---but pauses.
The moments where men stopped to talk, where

carts were delayed because a signature took longer than it should have,
where a door was left ajar because everyone involved assumed it would be
closed by someone else.

He learned which windows were opened for air and which were opened for
convenience. He learned which lights burned through the night and which
were extinguished as a matter of habit. He learned the sound of the
place when it was empty and the sound it made when it was lying about
being empty.

On the second night, he tested it.

A loose stone here. A shifted crate there. Nothing that would trip
alarms---nothing dramatic. Just enough to see who corrected the error
and how quickly.

The corrections came fast.

That didn't worry him.

What worried him was how quietly they came.

No shouting. No accusations. No scrambling. Just men and women stepping
into place and fixing things like they'd been told to expect it.

Kael didn't back out.

He adjusted.

On the fourth night, he went in.

He didn't climb. He didn't swim. He didn't do anything that would look
clever in hindsight. He walked with purpose through the service corridor
where carts were expected to move late and linger longer than they
should. He timed it so a clerk would be arguing with a driver about a
seal that had been broken the day before.

People who are arguing don't look up.

He slipped through the side door five feet behind a man carrying ledgers
and matched his pace for three steps before

peeling off into a shadow that hadn't been there a moment earlier.

Inside, the annex smelled like paper and oil and old salt. Lamps glowed
behind glass. The floors were swept but never clean. Every sound had a
reason.

Kael moved with the building, not against it. He crossed when carts
crossed. He paused when people paused. He slid sideways when a door
opened in front of him, then forward again when it closed.

He didn't rush.

That was the key.

Rushing was how you told a place you didn't belong.

The first obstacle was a narrow room with desks arranged to funnel
traffic past a single counter. The clerk stationed there had learned to
recognize faces, not intentions. Kael waited until a stack of papers
fell---accidentally, deliberately, it didn't matter---and stepped
through the gap while the clerk bent to retrieve them.

The second obstacle was a man with a badge he didn't understand and a
knife he hadn't used in years. Kael watched the man's posture, waited
for the moment his weight shifted to his bad knee, then crossed behind
him and left him standing there with the sense that something had just
gone wrong without knowing what.

Kael left no bodies behind him.

Not because he couldn't.

Because he didn't need to.

The annex opened into a long hall lined with doors that were never
locked because the locks were the doors themselves---numbers, schedules,
expectations. Kael traced them with his eyes until he found the one that
didn't quite belong.

The hinges were newer. The threshold had been replaced.

Someone had tried to make it invisible by making it better.

Kael slipped inside.

The room was smaller than he expected. No grand vault. No heavy chests.
Just shelves and tables and a single reinforced cabinet set into the
wall, its seams precise enough to suggest more care than strength.

Kael crossed the space and rested his hand on the cabinet.

He felt it immediately.

Not weight. Not resistance.

Significance.

This wasn't valuable because it was rare. It was valuable because it
moved things.

He opened it.

Inside was a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and twine, sealed with a stamp
he'd seen before---not often, but enough to recognize the pattern. The
cloth was marked with dates, not names. Corrections had been made in
careful script, numbers overwritten with numbers that pretended they'd
always been there.

Kael smiled.

He took the bundle and replaced it with something else of equal size and
lesser importance. He didn't rush the substitution. He matched the
placement exactly. He adjusted the cloth until the folds sat the same
way.

Then he closed the cabinet.

He didn't leave immediately.

He read.

Not everything. Just enough.

The bundle was a ledger, but not the kind that listed money. It listed
movement. Goods that passed through the city without belonging to it.
Delays that cost more than bribes. Names

that appeared only once, and then never again.

The boat appeared twice.

That made it a pattern.

Kael wrapped the ledger back up and secured it against his side. He
breathed once, slow and steady, and turned to leave.

That was when the hall shifted.

Not dramatically. Not alarmingly. Just enough that Kael felt it before
he saw it---the way air changes when a door opens behind you that wasn't
supposed to open yet.

Voices murmured.

Steps overlapped where they hadn't before.

Kael moved.

Five feet to the side, then forward, slipping through a space between
bodies that hadn't been there a second earlier. He kept the ledger
tight, his free hand open and ready.

A man glanced up as Kael passed and frowned---not in fear, but in
confusion. Kael reached out and pressed two fingers against the man's
throat, just enough to make him swallow hard and forget what he'd been
about to say.

Kael kept moving.

The exit he'd planned was clear.

That was the second thing that worried him.

He didn't take it.

Instead, he turned down a narrower passage that smelled of old ink and
damp stone, counted three doors, and stepped sideways through a gap that
led into a storage room where nothing important was ever kept.

He waited there, breathing quietly, until the sounds passed.

Then he left the annex the way he'd come in---unremarkable,
unremembered.

Outside, the night felt ordinary.

Kael crossed two bridges and three alleys before he stopped,

and when he did, it was only to rest his ankle and adjust the wrap. Pain
flared, then settled.

He was alive.

The ledger was secure.

The city hadn't reacted.

That was the third thing that worried him.

Kael didn't return to a safehouse. He went somewhere worse---a place
where people were loud and drunk and convinced nothing important ever
happened to them. He sat in a corner with his back to a wall and ordered
food he didn't finish.

No one bothered him.

No one followed.

When he finally moved again, it was to a room he'd rented under a name
that wasn't his. He barred the door, lit a single lamp, and set the
ledger on the table.

He studied it for a long time before opening it fully.

The handwriting changed three times across the pages. The dates
overlapped in ways that made sense only if you knew which schedules lied
by default. He traced the movement of goods from dock to annex to boat
and back again, noting the places where numbers didn't align unless
someone wanted them not to.

This wasn't just theft.

This was coordination.

Kael closed the ledger and wrapped it again.

He slept with it under his hand.

In the morning, a message waited for him.

Not at the door.

On the table.

He frowned, scanned the room, then picked it up.

The paper was blank except for a single line written in the

same careful hand he'd seen before.

Receipt acknowledged. Balance pending.

Kael smiled slowly.

They knew.

Not everything. Not yet.

But enough.

He folded the note and burned it, then packed what he needed and left
the room without looking back. Outside, Gravewater carried on, unaware
that anything had shifted.

But Kael felt it.

The job had gone too well.

And the city, which had once reacted by lashing out, had done something
far worse.

It had absorbed the impact.

Kael disappeared into the streets, ledger hidden, ankle aching, mind
already turning over the implications.

He hadn't lost.

Not yet.

But somewhere behind him, systems were aligning.

And soon---very soon---those margins he'd noticed at the start would
close.

CHAPTER 28

Kael learned something on the morning after the theft:

Gravewater didn't scream when you hurt it.

It logged you.

The city woke the way it always did---wet wood complaining underfoot,
gulls arguing with the sky, men hauling ropes like they'd been born
gripping them---but the currents underneath had shifted. Kael felt it in
the way a shopkeeper's eyes slid past him and then came back, slower. In
the way a clerk

hesitated before stamping a receipt, as if the stamp itself had become a
liability. In the way certain alleys that usually held three different
kinds of business were suddenly empty.

Not fear.

Procedure.

Kael kept moving.

He didn't go to his usual fence. He didn't go to his rented room. He
didn't take the straight path to anything. He took a looping route
across the mid-tiers and down toward the channels, crossing where boards
creaked loudest, moving through crowds thick enough to swallow a boy
with a limp.

The ledger stayed wrapped and tight against his ribs. Its weight was
wrong---not heavy, not awkward---just significant in a way that made the
world feel too aware of it.

He needed to turn it into coin or leverage before it turned into a
noose.

The document fence he'd used before was the obvious move.

So Kael didn't go there.

Instead he went to a tea shop that served the kind of bitter leaf-water
officials pretended to enjoy. He sat in the corner and watched the
street through steam. He listened to the rhythm of footsteps. He watched
who came and went, who lingered without ordering, who looked at hands
instead of faces.

A man entered who didn't belong.

Clean boots, not new. Coat cut well. Hands empty. Eyes trained to
memorize.

He didn't look around like he was nervous. He looked around like he was
counting.

Kael didn't move.

The man ordered tea, sat at a table with his back to the wall, and
opened a small folder as if it were the most normal thing

in the world to read in public. He didn't glance at Kael again.

Which meant Kael had already been seen.

Kael waited until the man lifted his cup, then slid sideways into the
flow of the shop---one table, one chair, one thin gap of space---and
left through the back without making the door creak.

Outside, the air felt heavier.

Kael moved faster.

Not running. Not panicking.

Just compressing time.

By noon he had confirmation: the annex theft had been processed. Not
solved, not avenged---processed, the way a counting house processed a
loss. A new line added to a ledger. A note passed along. A procedure
triggered.

He felt it when he passed a dock office and saw a board posted with
schedules.

Not his name.

Not even a warning.

Just a new line: AUDIT WINDOW: EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY.

The people reading it frowned, shrugged, and went back to work.

But the line meant someone from outside the usual chain had come in.

Auditors didn't carry knives.

Auditors carried consequences.

Kael turned away from the office and took the long route toward the
lower channels, where noise and stink made space for a boy who didn't
want to be remembered. He slipped past a fish stall and stopped just
long enough to listen.

Two dockhands argued near a barrel of nails.

"---I'm telling you, it wasn't Watch," one muttered. "Different coats.
Different way of talking."

"Everybody says that," the other replied.

"Nah. These ones got paper. Real paper. Like it matters."

Kael kept walking.

He didn't need the whole truth. He just needed the shape.

Someone had entered the board.

And that meant the theft was no longer a local problem.

It was a flow problem.

Which meant the people who lived off that flow were going to move.

Kael's plan had been simple: sell leverage fast, disappear into the next
job, let the city argue with itself while he stayed one step ahead. It
had worked because the city was slow to unify and too proud to admit it
didn't control everything.

Now it wasn't about pride.

Now it was about accounts.

And accounts never forgot.

He decided to force his payout.

The clean job had acknowledged receipt. Balance pending. That meant
someone expected him to come collect the rest---and likely expected to
measure what he did next.

Kael chose a meeting point that gave him three exits and a crowd.

A public market near the waterline where crates and bodies moved in
constant friction. He arrived early and blended in by acting like he
belonged. He found a place where he could see the approach routes, then
waited, chewing on a piece of bread that tasted like sawdust.

The man in gray arrived exactly when expected.

He didn't come alone.

That was the first wrongness.

Not bodyguards in the usual sense---no men with clubs glaring at
everyone---but two clerks, one on either side, each carrying a satchel.
They walked like people who had never been hit in the face but had
ruined lives with ink.

Kael felt the knowing stir. Not flaring, not screaming---just aligning
angles in his head.

The man in gray didn't smile. "You're punctual."

Kael nodded. "So are you."

The man gestured slightly, and one clerk stepped forward and produced a
pouch. Not thrown. Not slid. Placed in the open like a ritual offering.

Kael didn't touch it yet.

He kept his eyes on the other clerk.

That one held nothing out.

Just watched.

Kael said quietly, "You brought accounting."

The man in gray's eyes narrowed a fraction. "You brought a theft."

Kael shrugged. "Fair trade."

The man's voice stayed calm. "We've acknowledged receipt. We'll pay the
balance. Then you will return the original ledger."

Kael smiled faintly. "No."

The clerk on the right twitched.

The man in gray didn't. "That wasn't the agreement."

Kael's smile sharpened. "Your agreement was written for someone who dies
on the way out."

Silence. Market noise kept going. Someone shouted a price. Someone
cursed. Somewhere, a gull screamed.

The man in gray said, "You're making yourself expensive."

Kael leaned slightly forward. "I already am."

The man's gaze flicked over Kael's face. Over his limp. Over

the way he held his shoulders like someone who'd bled recently and
hadn't had time to complain about it.

"Then we'll do this properly," the man said.

He nodded once.

The clerk on the left opened his satchel and withdrew a stamped
document.

Kael didn't read it. He felt it.

It was a copy of something. A replication.

The clerk said, "Receipt of transfer. Your signature not required. Your
presence noted."

Kael's gut went cold.

Presence noted.

That was the botch beginning to breathe.

Kael's eyes flicked up, scanning the crowd.

He saw them.

Not in formation. Not closing in. Just placed---men leaning on posts,
women with baskets standing too still, a boy pretending to tie his shoe
in the same spot too long.

Watch? No.

These were not the city's blunt hands. These were the quiet ones.

The man in gray kept his voice level. "We pay you. You return the
ledger. You walk away. Or we log this as a refusal and proceed
accordingly."

Kael stared at him for a long beat.

Then he made his decision.

He took the pouch.

Coin clinked softly. Heavy. Enough to tempt.

He tucked it away.

He did not hand over the ledger.

Instead, he stepped sideways into the moving bodies of the

market, like he was simply leaving.

The man in gray didn't shout. Didn't signal a chase.

That was the second wrongness.

He let Kael go.

Kael moved faster.

He crossed a bridge, slipped down a stairwell, and disappeared into a
service alley that smelled like old fish and wet rope. He shifted again,
five feet here, five feet there---short jumps that kept him out of sight
lines, out of reach, out of predictability.

He didn't go to ground yet.

He needed to see the hook.

Because they hadn't chased him.

Which meant they were doing something worse.

They were letting him lead them.

Kael doubled back on his own trail, not directly, but in a wide loop
that returned him to a view of the market from above. He crouched on a
roofline, watching through gaps in boards.

The man in gray had not moved.

He stood where he'd stood, speaking quietly to the clerks.

Then the clerk on the right opened his satchel and withdrew a second
document.

Kael's breath caught.

They had prepared paperwork for multiple outcomes.

They weren't reacting.

They were running a script.

A runner approached the man in gray---a real runner, breathless,
frantic.

The man in gray listened, nodded once, and handed the runner the second
document.

The runner bolted.

Kael watched him go, then felt the knowing tighten around an

idea.

That runner wasn't going to chase Kael.

He was going to deliver a record of Kael.

To who?

Kael dropped off the roof and moved.

He didn't follow the runner directly. He cut angles, used rooftops, used
short displacements to eat distance without crossing open ground.

He caught up three streets later.

The runner ducked into a small office tucked behind a warehouse
door---one of those places where schedules lived and money moved without
being touched.

Kael reached the door as it closed.

He listened.

Ink scratch. Murmured voices. The runner's breath still loud.

Kael slipped sideways through a gap in the frame where the latch didn't
quite catch, sliding into the back room like a shadow with a heartbeat.

He saw the runner at a desk, handing over the document.

Across from him sat a woman with white hair tied back tight, eyes sharp.
Not a dock boss. Not Watch.

Something else.

She took the paper and didn't read it like a person reads.

She read it like a weapon.

Kael stepped forward.

The runner saw him first and choked on a gasp.

Kael was on him in two steps, one hand clamping the runner's mouth shut,
the other driving the knife in clean. The runner sagged.

Kael eased him down quietly.

The woman didn't scream.

She didn't reach for a weapon.

She reached for a bell on the desk.

Kael's stomach dropped.

That wasn't panic.

That was procedure.

Kael moved---five feet sideways, then forward---and slapped the bell
away before her fingers could ring it.

She didn't flinch.

She looked at Kael with calm, appraising eyes and said, "You're young."

The words landed like a blade.

Kael's jaw tightened. "Where's the copy."

She nodded toward the paper on the desk. "Already read."

Kael snatched it.

It wasn't just a receipt.

It was a description.

A pattern.

A list of incidents.

It linked Mireline Street. It linked the annex. It linked the market.

Not with name.

With method.

With timing.

With the kind of details only someone comparing reports could write.

Kael's throat went dry.

He looked up.

The woman said, softly, almost kindly, "You don't understand what you've
done."

Kael hissed, "You don't understand what I'll do."

She didn't blink. "I understand enough to know you can't erase paper."

Footsteps sounded outside.

Not running. Not shouting.

Approaching.

Kael's mind snapped cold and clear.

He could kill her.

He could burn the office.

He could vanish and leave bodies.

And the paper would still exist somewhere else, because people like this
never held the only copy.

He had misread the board.

Not himself.

The knowing pressed, showing him angles, exits, the distance to the back
wall, the thin window that opened onto an alley.

Kael grabbed the paper, shoved it into the lantern flame, and watched it
curl. Not enough. Not fast enough.

The woman said, "There are more."

Kael's stomach turned.

He stepped backward.

The door to the office opened.

Two men entered.

Not Watch. No uniforms.

They moved like men trained to take someone alive, not like thugs trying
to win a fight. Their eyes flicked across the room, took in the dead
runner, the burning paper, Kael's knife.

They didn't hesitate.

One stepped left, the other right, cutting angles.

Kael didn't fight them head-on.

He slipped sideways into space, appearing behind the left man and
driving his blade into the kidney. The man grunted, stumbled, but didn't
collapse---tougher than a dockhand,

disciplined enough to keep moving.

The second man lunged.

Kael vanished five feet, reappearing behind him, and slashed across the
back of his leg. The man dropped to one knee, teeth clenched.

Kael didn't finish him.

He couldn't afford time.

More steps sounded outside.

More procedure.

More bodies.

Kael grabbed the burning remains of the paper and shoved them into the
woman's ink tray, turning the ink into sludge. He kicked the lantern off
the desk so the flame spilled onto loose ledgers and dry paper.

Fire would buy him minutes.

Fire would also draw attention.

He didn't have a better option.

Kael slipped through the narrow window into the alley as the room began
to smoke, landing hard enough that his ankle screamed. Pain flared
white. He gritted his teeth and moved anyway, disappearing into the line
between two buildings and then stepping sideways again---five feet, five
feet, five feet---until he was out of sight and the alley had become
just another wet cut in the city.

He didn't stop until he reached a lower channel bridge and leaned
against the rail, chest heaving, hands shaking.

He wasn't shaking from fear.

He was shaking from understanding.

He had been fighting men.

He had been fighting traps.

He had been fighting money.

Now he was fighting memory.

And memory didn't bleed.

He wiped his blade on his sleeve and forced himself to think.

The woman's face stayed in his mind. Not her threat. Not her calm.

Her words.

You're young.

Kael swallowed hard and looked down at his hands.

They looked like a man's hands when they were covered in blood.

But they were still a boy's hands when they were clean.

He took a breath that hurt his ribs and moved again.

He couldn't go to his usual fences. He couldn't sleep in any room tied
to his patterns. He couldn't keep moving openly, because now the city
didn't need to catch him once.

It just needed to confirm he existed again.

Kael needed a place that didn't ask questions, didn't log names, didn't
care what you'd done as long as you paid.

A place with its own rules.

A place where men came and went without being recorded because being
recorded would destroy the business.

He limped through the lower tiers toward a street he knew by smell
before he saw it.

Perfume, fried oil, sweat, and clean soap---the strange mixture of
comfort and transaction.

The brothel's door was open, guarded by a man with arms like rope and
eyes like a locked door. He watched Kael approach.

Kael didn't hide his limp.

He didn't hide his tiredness.

He didn't hide the fact that his clothes were damp, that his sleeve was
stained, that his eyes were too old for his face.

He stopped two paces from the guard.

"I need a room," Kael said quietly. "A week. Maybe more."

The guard's gaze flicked over him, assessing like everyone did now---but
with a different goal. Not to judge. To decide if Kael would bring
trouble that would spill into the house.

Kael reached into his pocket and set coin into the guard's palm.

Enough to make the guard's eyebrows rise.

"Front," Kael added. "No name. No visitors unless I say."

The guard weighed the coin, then looked at Kael again.

There was a pause---long enough that Kael felt the knowing tighten,
