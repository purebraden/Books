CHAPTER 13

The lower levels of Gravewater were not built for living.

They were built for holding.

Kael felt it in his bones long before he could have explained it. The
air was heavier down here, not only with damp and salt but with the
weight of purpose. The boards beneath his boots were thicker, reinforced
with iron plates bolted on in desperate layers. The support beams were
too close together, crowding the corridors like ribs around a fragile
heart. Everywhere Kael looked, the city's structure was doubled, then
tripled, then bound again---as if the original builders had realized too
late what they'd been asked to carry.

The light was different too. Lanterns hung at measured intervals, their
flames low and steady, protected by cages of wire. Not to keep the wind
off. To keep the light from reaching too far.

Kael moved carefully, one hand on the railing whenever it

was available. His ankle still ached from the jump, pain sharp enough to
keep him present in his body---which was the only way to keep the
knowing from opening fully.

Even so, it pressed.

It wanted him to see the lines beneath everything: what held the pylons,
what pulled the counterweights, what was being traded for stability. The
pressure behind his eyes throbbed steadily, like a warning bell muted by
distance.

He rounded a corner and stepped into a space that made him stop.

A great chamber opened up beneath the city---part maintenance hall, part
engineered void. Thick chains ran overhead, disappearing into darkness
above. Counterweights the size of small houses hung suspended in cradles
of iron, swaying almost imperceptibly with the movement of water below.
Huge braces arched across the space, old wood scarred with tool marks
and patched with newer metal like splints over broken bones.

It was beautiful in the way a desperate thing could be
beautiful---craftsmanship layered with survival.

Kael stared, breath held.

This wasn't decay.

This was deliberate.

A shout broke the silence.

Kael flinched and ducked instinctively behind a stack of spare timbers,
keeping his body hidden while his eyes tracked movement.

A crew of workers moved through the chamber in a tight line, hauling a
length of chain thicker than Kael's arm. Their clothes were stained dark
with damp and rust. Their faces were drawn, eyes shadowed with
exhaustion. They moved

with the grim coordination of people who did this every day and hated it
every day.

Two Watchmen walked with them---not alongside, not helping, but present.
Supervising.

One of the workers stumbled. The chain jerked, forcing the line to halt.
The man caught himself on a brace, breathing hard.

A Watchman snapped, "Move."

The worker tried. His hands slipped. The chain lurched again, pulling at
the others. A second worker swore, yanking to compensate.

Kael felt the knowing surge, hot and insistent, as if sensing the
imbalance in the moment.

He resisted, but it bled through anyway---enough to show him the shape
of what was happening without fully opening.

This wasn't just labor.

It was payment.

The counterweights above shifted slightly, reacting to the chain's
movement. The braces groaned softly. Somewhere below, unseen, water
pulled inward.

Kael swallowed, throat tight.

The worker who had stumbled looked up briefly, eyes unfocused with
fatigue. For a heartbeat, his gaze met Kael's hiding place---and then
slid away, as if he'd seen a ghost he couldn't afford to acknowledge.

He didn't call out.

He didn't warn anyone.

He just kept moving, because stopping would cost more than he had left.

Kael felt a cold anger rise in him. Not at the worker. Not even at the
Watchmen, whose cruelty was lazy and routine.

At the system that made this normal.

The knowing pressed harder. Kael's vision sharpened unwillingly, pain
blooming behind his eyes.

He saw debts.

Not coins. Not paper.

Pressure lines.

He saw how the workers' exhaustion was not incidental but accounted
for---how their bodies, their hours, their mistakes were part of the
calculation. He saw the Watchmen's authority tethered upward into the
unseen structure that had offered him a "choice" in a warehouse lit too
evenly.

He saw the city as a machine that ran on human strain.

Kael clenched his teeth and forced his gaze down, nails biting into his
palms until the pain grounded him.

The worker line passed. The chain slid into place with a heavy metallic
clunk. The counterweight above swayed once, then steadied. The chamber
exhaled, wood and iron settling under redistributed load.

One of the Watchmen glanced around as if bored, then moved on.

Kael waited until their footsteps faded.

When he finally moved again, his hands were shaking.

He followed a corridor that sloped downward, the air growing colder with
each step. The sound of water became more pronounced---no longer distant
slapping but a deep, slow movement below the boards, like breath drawn
through a vast lung.

He reached a junction marked by symbols scratched into the wood---simple
shapes, repeated, worn down by time. Not official signage. Worker marks.
Warnings. Directions. This way holds.

A narrow passage branched off to the left, partially blocked

by a hanging tarp. Kael could hear voices beyond it---workers again,
speaking in low tones.

He hesitated.

The knowing pulsed at the edge of his awareness, showing him the tension
in the air like static before lightning. There was trouble ahead. Maybe
nothing immediate. Maybe something worse.

He crept closer and peered through a gap in the tarp.

Two workers stood over a third who was sitting on the floor, back
against the wall, breathing shallowly. His face was pale. His hands
trembled. He looked like he'd been wrung out.

"We can't carry him," one worker muttered. "Shift changes in ten.
They'll dock us."

The other worker's mouth tightened. "If we leave him here, he'll drown
when the surge comes."

The first worker flinched. "Then he shouldn't have collapsed."

Kael felt something in his chest twist.

The knowing surged violently, pulling at his eyes, demanding he see what
it already half showed him: the worker's collapse was predictable.
Accounted for. The system would replace him. The surge would come
regardless.

Kael's head throbbed hard enough that he had to grip the wall to stay
upright.

He had a choice.

Intervene---and draw attention. Create a disruption. Become visible.

Or pass through---stay hidden. Preserve himself. Let the machine
continue.

Kael thought of the bread he'd been given. The debt he carried now
because someone had chosen a small kindness in defiance of the city's
quiet punishment. He thought of

Mirek's body left to the water. Of Tessa swallowed whole.

He hated how quickly he could rationalize walking away.

Kael stepped through the tarp.

The workers jerked in surprise. One reached for a tool like a weapon.

"Don't," Kael said hoarsely. "I'm not Watch."

They stared at him.

Kael crouched beside the collapsed man. His skin was cold and damp.
Breathing shallow.

"Can you stand?" Kael asked.

The man's eyes fluttered open, unfocused. He tried to speak and
couldn't.

Kael looked at the other two. "Help me."

One worker shook his head violently. "We can't. They'll---"

"I know," Kael snapped, then forced his voice down. "They'll dock you.
They'll punish you. But if you leave him, he dies."

The workers hesitated, trapped between procedure and humanity.

Kael felt the knowing press hard, urging him to see which choice they
would make. He refused it. He didn't want to know. Knowing wouldn't make
them better.

Finally, one of them swore and stepped forward. "Fine. But if they catch
us---"

"They won't," Kael lied, and hated how easily it came now.

Together they hauled the collapsed man up. His weight sagged heavily,
nearly pulling them all down. Kael's ankle screamed in protest. He
ignored it.

They moved quickly, half-dragging, half-carrying, toward a side passage
that smelled drier. Kael kept his head down, willing the knowing to stay
quiet.

They reached a small alcove reinforced with iron grating over the
floor---higher, safer. The workers eased the man down,

breathing hard.

The collapsed man coughed once, weakly, then drew a deeper breath.

One worker stared at Kael. "Who are you?"

Kael didn't answer. Names were hooks.

Footsteps echoed faintly somewhere nearby.

The workers stiffened, panic flashing. Kael's pulse spiked.

"You need to go," Kael said quickly. "Both of you. Now."

"But---" one began.

"Now," Kael repeated.

They didn't argue. They fled down the corridor, boots thudding softly.

Kael stood alone in the alcove, listening.

The footsteps grew louder---measured, unhurried. Watch pace.

Kael's head throbbed, and the knowing surged in response, eager to map
escape routes, to show him exactly how the trap would close.

Kael turned away from the alcove and moved deeper down the corridor.

Behind him, the passage he'd come through shuddered.

A heavy metallic sound rang out---iron meeting iron.

Kael spun back in time to see an old gate sliding down, dropped from
above. It hit the floor with a final clang, cutting off the route
upward.

For a moment Kael simply stared.

Then he laughed once, quietly, because it was either that or scream.

The city had watched him choose compassion.

And it had answered by closing the door behind him.

Kael turned away from the sealed gate, breathing hard, and

continued into the deeper containment corridors where the air grew
colder and the water's pull became undeniable.

There was no easy return now.

Only forward, into the weight the city had been paying for.

CHAPTER 14

The corridor narrowed until Kael had to turn his shoulders sideways to
pass.

The iron bands along the walls were closer together here, bolted so
densely that they resembled scales. They hummed faintly---not
constantly, but in pulses, as if responding to something deeper than
sound. Kael felt the vibration through his boots and into his bones, a
slow rhythm that matched neither his breathing nor his heart.

The knowing pressed down on him like a hand between his shoulder blades.

Not urging him forward.

Measuring him.

He moved carefully, each step deliberate. The pain behind his eyes had
changed quality---not sharper, not more intense, but heavier, as if his
skull were bearing weight rather than heat. When he passed beneath a
thick arch of reinforced beams, the pressure deepened, then eased again
as he cleared it.

The system reacted to his presence.

Kael swallowed and kept going.

Ahead, the corridor opened into another chamber---smaller than the great
maintenance hall above, but more precise. This space had been built by
people who understood exactly what they were asking it to do. Thick
pylons anchored the floor, disappearing through iron grates into the
water below. Between them ran chains, counterbalanced by massive

drums inset into the walls.

And people.

Four workers stood at a control station along one side, hands on levers,
eyes fixed on gauges that twitched in small, constant increments. They
froze as Kael entered.

No one shouted.

No one reached for a weapon.

One of the workers---a woman with hair gone almost entirely
gray---exhaled slowly. "You're not on the slate."

Kael didn't answer. His mouth felt dry.

The knowing stirred, and he let it open just enough to read the room.

The workers' fear was real, but muted---contained by habit. Their
obligations ran upward into the same invisible structure Kael had sensed
before, but here the lines were thicker, heavier. These people didn't
just obey; they maintained.

"We've got a gap," another worker said quietly, eyes flicking to a gauge
that trembled too far toward red. "Shift replacement never arrived."

The gray-haired woman glanced at Kael again, then back to the gauge. "Of
course they didn't."

Kael felt the knowing throb sharply, a warning.

He stepped back instinctively. "I'm not---"

A deep groan rolled through the chamber, vibrating the iron bands. The
floor shuddered. One of the gauges spiked violently, its needle slamming
against the stop with a sharp clack.

The gray-haired woman swore. "We're slipping."

Kael's vision blurred as the knowing flared, flooding him with
structural awareness. He saw pressure building below---water pulling
inward harder than before, iron straining,

counterweights shifting out of balance.

He saw the absence.

A missing load.

The system wasn't failing because it was broken.

It was failing because someone wasn't there.

"Get back," Kael said hoarsely, backing toward the exit.

The woman shook her head. "If we disengage now, the surge will propagate
upward. Mid-tiers won't hold."

Kael stared at her. "Then disengage somewhere else."

She met his gaze evenly. "There is nowhere else."

Another groan rippled through the chamber. The hum deepened, vibrating
Kael's teeth.

The knowing pressed harder, heavier than it ever had before. It wasn't
showing him lies or intentions now. It was showing him load---where
weight should be, where it wasn't, and what would happen if it remained
absent.

A human-shaped absence.

Kael's stomach lurched. "You want me to---"

"We want someone to," the woman said, not unkindly. "You're here."

Kael laughed weakly. "That's not a qualification."

One of the other workers glanced between them, panic creeping into his
voice. "The bracket won't hold much longer."

The gray-haired woman looked at Kael again. Her eyes were tired, not
cruel. "You can leave," she said. "No one will stop you."

The knowing flared brutally at her words, showing Kael the truth beneath
them.

If he left, the system would compensate---violently. The surge would
ripple upward, snapping weaker supports, drowning workers who never even
saw the water move wrong. The city would call it an accident.

Kael staggered, gripping a pylon to stay upright. His head felt like it
was splitting open from the inside, pressure and pain grinding together.

He didn't want this.

He didn't want to be part of it.

But the city had already decided.

"Where?" Kael asked quietly.

The woman gestured toward a narrow platform extending over the grating.
A harness hung there, heavy and old, leather darkened by sweat and
water. Chains ran from it into the wall, disappearing into the machinery
beyond.

"Just long enough to stabilize," she said. "Until the next shift
arrives."

Kael stared at the harness.

The knowing surged again, heavier than before, showing him the truth
without mercy: long enough was a variable the system adjusted when
convenient.

Kael's hands trembled as he stepped onto the platform. The iron grating
beneath his boots vibrated, responding to his weight.

The hum shifted.

The gauges steadied slightly.

Everyone in the room felt it.

Kael swallowed hard. "I don't know what I'm doing."

The woman shook her head. "Neither did the last one."

Kael flinched. "What happened to them?"

She didn't answer.

That was answer enough.

Kael slipped the harness over his shoulders, the leather stiff and cold
against his skin. The chains clinked softly as he adjusted it, sound
swallowed quickly by the chamber's

steady hum.

The knowing pressed down on him like a physical thing now, aligning with
the machinery, sinking into the same rhythm. Kael felt himself
register---not as a person, but as mass. Load. Resistance.

The platform shuddered, then steadied.

The gauges eased further toward green.

A collective breath went through the room.

Kael closed his eyes, nausea rolling through him. His head pounded
mercilessly, pain blooming and receding in time with the hum. He felt
the water below pulling, testing, as if curious about this new weight it
could feel above.

"Stay still," the woman said gently. "Let the system adjust."

Kael laughed once, hollow. "That's been the story all along, hasn't it?"

She didn't deny it.

Minutes passed. Or seconds. Kael couldn't tell. Time felt distorted
here, stretched thin by pressure and pain.

Slowly, the machinery settled into a new equilibrium. The hum softened.
The gauges stabilized.

Kael opened his eyes and saw the workers watching him---not with
hostility, not even pity, but with a weary gratitude that made his
stomach twist.

The city had accepted him.

He felt it then, unmistakably: his presence reduced strain elsewhere.
Somewhere above, a brace stopped groaning. Somewhere below, the water's
pull eased just a fraction.

Leaving now would undo that.

Leaving would cost lives.

Kael sagged against the harness, exhaustion crashing over him like a
wave. He was shaking uncontrollably now,

muscles screaming from tension he hadn't realized he was holding.

"I didn't agree to this," he whispered.

The knowing pulsed, heavy and undeniable.

Consent had never been part of the equation.

The gray-haired woman met his gaze. "We'll call for replacement," she
said. "As soon as we can."

Kael believed her.

He also knew the system might decide he was efficient.

He stared out over the grating, down into the darkness where the water
moved wrong, and felt the terrible truth settle in his bones:

The city didn't just want him contained.

It wanted him useful.

And for the first time since Tessa died, Kael didn't know how to leave
without killing someone else in his place.

CHAPTER 15

At first, Kael told himself it would be minutes.

He watched the gauges. He listened to the hum. He counted breaths to
keep the knowing from opening wider than he could survive. The workers
moved around him with efficient care, checking chains, adjusting levers,
making small corrections that kept the entire chamber from slipping into
catastrophe.

Minutes, he insisted. Until the next shift.

Then the gray-haired woman came back with a cup of water.

Not offered like charity. Not presented like comfort. Placed on a crate
within reach of his bound hands, as if fueling a machine that had begun
to run hot.

"Drink," she said.

Kael stared at the cup.

His throat burned with thirst. His mouth tasted like blood and iron. He
wanted to refuse out of spite.

He drank anyway.

The water was stale and faintly metallic, but it soothed the rawness
enough that he could breathe without tasting copper.

"How long?" Kael asked, voice hoarse.

The woman looked at a gauge, then at him. "As long as it takes."

Kael laughed once, quietly. "That's not an answer."

She didn't disagree. She simply moved away, returning to her station
like a person returning to duty.

Time blurred.

It didn't pass in clean segments down here. It accumulated like
damp---slow, clinging, thickening in seams until suddenly everything
felt heavier than before. The chamber's light never changed. The hum
never stopped. The water below never ceased its wrong, patient pull.

Kael's muscles began to cramp within an hour. His shoulders burned where
the harness bit into him. His legs trembled from holding still against
the subtle sway of counterweights overhead. The system demanded
steadiness, and his body was not designed to be a static component.

He shifted once, a fraction.

The hum deepened.

A gauge twitched toward red.

The gray-haired woman's head snapped up. "Still."

Kael froze. Pain lanced through his ankle and up his calf. He wanted to
scream. He didn't, because screaming was movement, and movement was
strain.

He swallowed it down and stared at the iron grating beneath him, where
darkness pooled like ink.

The knowing pressed into him constantly now, not trying to show him
secrets but aligning him to the system's rhythm. It was less like sight
and more like load awareness---a sense of how weight traveled through
braces and chains, where it concentrated, where it threatened to
fracture.

He could feel stress as clearly as he used to feel hunger.

That terrified him more than the pain.

A worker approached during what Kael guessed was a shift change---though
nothing in the chamber signaled it except the arrival of different
faces.

A younger man this time, eyes too bright with nervousness, held out
another cup of water.

"Here," he said, not meeting Kael's gaze.

Kael took it carefully. The chain of the harness clinked, and the hum
responded with a faint change, as if the chamber noticed even that small
sound.

The worker flinched. "Sorry."

Kael swallowed. "How many shifts have passed?"

The worker hesitated. Then he shrugged without commitment. "Enough."

Kael stared at him. "Is there no replacement?"

The worker's mouth tightened. "They said one was coming."

"They," Kael echoed softly.

The knowing surged at the word, tugging upward through the layers of
obligation Kael could now sense like stacked stone. He saw the chain of
command as a structural feature, not a hierarchy: pressure moved
downward; blame moved nowhere.

Kael's head throbbed. He shut the knowing down as best he could.

The worker backed away quickly, as if proximity itself was dangerous.

Kael watched him go and felt something settle in his gut: he was no
longer being treated as a person trapped in a bad position.

He was being treated as a component under load.

The workers did small things for him---water, a strip of cloth pressed
against his bleeding nose, a brief adjustment to the harness so it
didn't cut as deeply into his collarbones. None of it felt like
kindness. It felt like maintenance.

No one apologized.

Apologies implied wrongness.

This was procedure.

At some point---Kael wasn't sure when---his hands began to tremble. Not
from fear. From fatigue. The shake traveled up his arms, into his
shoulders, becoming a subtle vibration that threatened to spread through
the harness.

Kael fought it with breath. With clenched jaw. With the sharp focus of a
man holding a fragile thing over a long drop.

The knowing pressed harder, almost angry at his weakness.

He felt it then, clearly: the system had begun to rely on his presence.
It had adjusted to him the way it adjusted to counterweights. His
steadiness became part of the equilibrium.

If he left suddenly, the chamber would react violently.

If he collapsed, it would react even worse.

He was a living brace.

And living braces fail.

The gray-haired woman returned again, carrying something wrapped in
cloth.

Food.

Hard bread, maybe, and a strip of dried fish. Not much. Enough to keep
his body from shutting down.

Kael's eyes narrowed. "Now you feed me."

She didn't look offended. "We keep the load stable."

Kael swallowed. The bread was dry enough to scrape his throat raw. He
ate anyway, because hunger made shaking worse.

He forced himself to speak between bites. "What happened to the last
one?"

The gray-haired woman didn't answer immediately.

That silence was heavier than any word.

Kael's pulse quickened. "Tell me."

She looked at him then, eyes flat with exhaustion. "He held longer than
he should have."

Kael felt cold spread through his chest. "And?"

"And the replacement was delayed," she said quietly. "Because delay is
cheaper than doing it right."

Kael stared at her, breath shallow. "He died."

The woman's jaw tightened. She didn't deny it.

Kael's stomach lurched. The knowing surged violently, showing him the
missing presence like a dark shape in the system's memory---an absence
that had once been a person.

Kael gagged, fighting bile.

The woman's voice was almost gentle. "Stay still."

Kael laughed---small, broken. "That's what you said to him too, wasn't
it?"

The woman didn't answer.

That silence was confirmation.

Kael closed his eyes and tried to steady his breathing. The chamber's
hum continued, indifferent. Above, counterweights swayed slowly. Below,
water pulled inward

with patient certainty.

Kael understood then, with awful clarity:

They weren't waiting for a replacement.

They were waiting to see how long Kael would hold.

If he held long enough, the system would decide he was efficient.

If he held too long, he would die, and they would replace him with
someone else---another worker, another body, another debt paid in flesh.

Kael opened his eyes.

His gaze fell on the levers at the control station. The chains. The
counterweight drums inset into the walls. The iron braces that
distributed load with brutal precision.

The knowing pulsed, offering him something he didn't want: the map of
the system.

It showed him weak points---places where a small shift would
redistribute load upward rather than down. Places where a gate could be
opened or closed to redirect pressure through a different corridor.
Places where the system could be forced to compensate elsewhere.

It also showed him the cost.

Every reroute meant strain somewhere else. Every shift meant someone
else had to bear weight.

Kael felt his throat tighten. His hands trembled harder.

He could attempt an escape.

But if he simply unhooked himself and ran, the chamber would buck. The
surge would propagate. Workers would die. Maybe hundreds above, if the
mid-tier braces snapped.

He could not leave cleanly.

He would have to choose where the weight went.

Kael stared at the iron grating beneath him, at the darkness where the
water moved wrong. His head pulsed with pain

and structural awareness in equal measure.

He thought of Mirek. Replaced by silence.

He thought of Tessa. Replaced by water.

He thought of Bram. Replaced by distance.

Gravewater replaced what it broke. Always.

Kael swallowed hard.

Then, slowly, he began to plan.

Not how to survive.

How to leave in a way the system couldn't ignore---without turning some
nameless worker into the next missing load.

His mouth tasted like blood.

His body shook.

And the knowing, heavy and relentless, showed him exactly how much
anything would cost.

CHAPTER 16

Kael did nothing at first.

That was the hardest part.

He held still in the harness, muscles shaking, breath shallow, letting
the room continue as it had been trained to continue. The workers moved
in their familiar loops---check, record, adjust, repeat. No one looked
at him directly anymore. He was present in the way a column was present.
Necessary. Unremarkable.

That was the weakness.

Procedure depended on expectation.

Kael waited until the gray-haired woman stepped away from the gauges to
confer with another worker. He waited until the younger man took her
place, eyes flicking between dials with practiced anxiety. He waited
until the hum of the chamber

settled into its most stable register---the sound it made when no one
expected anything to change.

Only then did Kael move.

Not much. Just enough.

He shifted his weight by a fraction, letting the harness take it, then
eased it back. The change was subtle enough that the gauges didn't
spike---just trembled. The younger worker frowned, tapped the glass of
one dial, and made a small adjustment.

Exactly as Kael needed.

The knowing pressed in, heavy but controlled, not showing him truths so
much as tolerances. He could feel the margins---how much variance the
procedure allowed before escalation. How long an anomaly could persist
before it became someone's responsibility.

Responsibility was the bottleneck.

Kael repeated the movement minutes later. A little more this time.
Enough to require a note.

The worker scribbled something on a slate and glanced toward the
corridor that led upward, as if expecting someone to appear and tell him
what to do.

No one came.

Kael felt sweat run down his spine. His arms trembled harder now, pain
flaring where the harness bit into him. The knowing throbbed dully, like
a bruise pressed too often.

He shifted again.

This time, the gray-haired woman noticed.

"Hold," she snapped.

Kael froze.

The gauges steadied. The hum settled.

She stared at the readouts, lips pressed thin. "That shouldn't have
fluctuated."

Kael said nothing. He kept his breathing shallow, controlled.

The woman looked at him---not accusing, not suspicious. Assessing.

"Are you slipping?" she asked.

Kael swallowed. "I'm tired."

That, at least, was allowed.

She nodded once. "We'll need to log it."

Log it.

Not stop it. Not change course. Record deviation and move on.

The knowing pulsed---this was the opening.

Kael waited until she turned away again, attention pulled by another
worker calling out a minor discrepancy on the far side of the chamber.

Then he acted.

He didn't touch the levers. He didn't interfere with the chains. He did
the only thing the procedure hadn't accounted for.

He spoke.

"If I go down," Kael said hoarsely, loud enough to carry, "you won't
have time to compensate."

The room stilled.

Every worker turned toward him.

Kael felt his heart hammering against his ribs. The knowing surged
painfully, mapping reactions, predicting responses, showing him exactly
how fragile this moment was.

The gray-haired woman stepped closer. "What are you doing?"

"Explaining," Kael said. "What happens if I lose consciousness. Or
muscle control. Or balance."

She stared at him. "That won't happen."

Kael laughed, sharp and humorless. "It already is."

As if on cue, his arms shook violently. Pain lanced through his
shoulders. He let a small sound escape---real, uncontrolled.

The younger worker swore under his breath.

Kael pressed on. "Your procedure assumes a static load. I'm not static.
I'm exhausted. And if I fail, you don't get a warning spike. You get a
cascade."

The gray-haired woman hesitated.

That hesitation was everything.

She looked at the gauges again, then at Kael, then at the corridor
leading upward.

"Who authorized this duration?" she demanded.

No one answered.

Because no one had.

It was the gap Kael needed.

"You don't have a replacement," Kael said quietly. "You're hoping delay
solves it. It won't."

Silence stretched.

The knowing burned behind his eyes, screaming at him to hold, to
maintain alignment, to not let the load slip while he talked.

Kael felt something tear in his shoulder. He bit back a cry.

The gray-haired woman cursed softly.

"Get the slate," she snapped to the younger worker. "Now."

The worker ran.

This was it.

Not escape. Escalation.

The woman looked back at Kael. "If I disengage you without clearance---"

"You'll write a report," Kael said. "And someone above you will have to
acknowledge it."

She stared at him, eyes sharp now. "You planned this."

Kael swallowed. "I planned for you to notice."

The slate came back. The woman skimmed it quickly, jaw tight.

She made a decision---not a moral one. A procedural one.

"All right," she said. "Temporary redistribution. Five minutes."

Five minutes was an eternity.

She barked orders. Workers moved fast now, urgency snapping into place
where habit had been. Chains adjusted. Counterweights shifted---not
smoothly, but adequately. The chamber groaned in protest.

Kael felt the load ease slightly.

"Unhook him," the woman said.

Hands grabbed the harness. The release mechanism stuck for a terrifying
second, then gave way with a harsh metallic snap.

Kael collapsed forward, barely caught by two workers who weren't
expecting his full weight.

The hum spiked, then steadied again---louder, rougher, but holding.
